The moment you realise you are not sure where you are, stop walking. The urge to press on and recognise something is what turns briefly unsure into genuinely lost.

## Sit down

Literally. Take the pack off, drink something, eat something. Ten minutes of this does more for your decision-making than an hour of walking, because low blood sugar and mild dehydration degrade judgement before you notice.

## Then think

- When were you last certain of your position?
- How long ago, and roughly what direction have you walked since?
- Can you see any landmark you recognise — a ridge line, a river, a road?
- Does anyone know your route and when you are due back?

That last question decides the next one.

## Stay put, or move?

**Stay put if** someone knows your route and expects you back, you have shelter and water, or the light is going. This is the right answer far more often than people expect. Searches that start from a known route are short.

**Consider moving if** nobody knows where you are, or you can actually see your destination — not guess at it, see it.

If you do move, pick one direction and hold it. Following water downstream tends toward people, at the cost of energy and daylight. Mark your route as you go so you can retrace it.

## Make yourself findable

Bright kit spread out, geometric shapes, [signalling in threes](/articles/how-to-signal-for-help-in-the-wilderness/). Get big rather than loud.

## Get warm before you get worried

Exposure works faster than thirst and much faster than hunger. Out of the wind, off the ground, dry layers on. [Shelter](/articles/how-to-build-an-emergency-shelter/) comes before almost everything else on the list.

## Retrace only if you can do it exactly

Backtracking works when you can genuinely follow your own route — clear prints, a distinctive path, features you actually recognise in the right order. It fails badly when it becomes guessing, because a wrong retrace moves you further from the last place anyone would look for you.

If you cannot retrace with confidence, staying put is almost always the better call.

## What your phone can do without signal

The GPS chip in a phone works without any network connection. That means the blue dot on a downloaded offline map still shows where you are, even with no bars at all — which is why downloading the map before you leave matters so much more than people expect.

Three things worth doing before you set off, so they are there when you need them:

- **Download the offline map** for the area, not just the route.
- **Screenshot the route and the nearest roads.** A screenshot survives everything.
- **Find your phone's emergency SOS function** and know how it works.

And in the moment: put the phone in aeroplane mode between attempts. Hunting for a network it cannot find is what flattens the battery, and cold flattens it faster still — so keep it in an inside pocket.

## If it is going to get dark

Stop early enough to prepare in daylight. Deciding to make camp with forty minutes of light left is a good decision; being forced to at dusk is not.

Use that time in this order: get out of the wind, insulate underneath you, put every dry layer on, then eat. Set something bright outside where it can be seen, and keep the whistle in your hand rather than in the pack.

Night is long and cold, and it passes. Almost nobody comes to harm from spending one uncomfortable night out. People come to harm from walking through it in rough ground.

## If you are with children

Keep everyone together and keep them warm and fed before anything else — children lose heat faster than adults and will not reliably say they are cold.

Give them a job. Collecting dry material, laying out bright kit, being in charge of the whistle. It is genuinely useful and it is also the thing that keeps a frightened child calm.

Teach them the rule that matters most before you ever need it: **if you are lost, stay where you are and make noise.** A child who stays put is found quickly. A child who wanders is the hardest search there is.

## What not to do

Do not walk after dark in rough ground. Do not split the group. Do not push on to "just have a look" past the point where turning back is easy. And do not start foraging — [food is the last priority](/articles/how-long-can-you-survive-without-food/), and the risk is real.
