Cold-weather camping needs two distinct clothing systems, not one: an active daytime layering setup for hiking or setting up camp, and a completely separate, warmer, dry sleep system for the night — treating them as the same thing is the most common reason people end up cold after dark.

## Daytime system

The standard base/mid/shell layers apply, adjusted for cold: a warmer base layer, real insulation in the mid layer, and a windproof shell. Manage sweat actively during setup and hiking — physical activity in cold weather still makes you sweat, and damp base layers at camp are a fast route to a cold evening.

## The camp-only warm layer

Once you stop moving, your heat production drops sharply, and cold-weather camping calls for a dedicated insulated jacket you only put on at camp — often warmer than anything you'd wear while actively hiking, because you're no longer generating heat to offset it.

## The sleep system, kept completely separate

Change into dry, dedicated sleep clothes before getting into your sleeping bag, even if your daytime layers feel only slightly damp. A small amount of moisture in a sleeping bag over a full cold night is enough to noticeably reduce how warm you sleep. Pack sleep clothes in their own dry bag so they never sit against wet daytime gear.

## Extremities at camp

Hands and feet cool fastest once you're sedentary. A warm hat, insulated camp booties or dry wool socks, and gloves you can wear while handling cold gear (tent poles, cook systems) make evenings meaningfully more comfortable.

## The core habit

Treat "what I hike in" and "what I sleep in" as two separate systems, not one outfit worn all day and night. That single separation prevents most of the cold-camping discomfort beginners report.
