Cold-weather hiking clothing means the same base/mid/shell system as any other hike, with two changes: a warmer mid layer and real attention to your extremities — head, hands, and feet lose heat disproportionately fast and are the first place cold weather actually goes wrong.

## Core layers for cold conditions

- **Base layer:** a heavier-weight merino or synthetic base than you'd wear in summer — still moisture-wicking, just thicker.
- **Mid layer:** a real insulator — fleece or a synthetic/down puffy — sized to layer over your base without restricting movement.
- **Shell:** windproof at minimum, waterproof if snow or rain is possible. Wind chill does more damage in cold weather than raw temperature.

One structural change is worth making in the cold: split the insulation in two. A thin fleece you can walk in, plus a packable puffy that only comes out when you stop, beats a single thick jacket that is too warm for climbing and not warm enough for standing around. You get two settings instead of one, and the one you need most — warmth while stationary — is the one a single mid layer handles worst.

## The extremities most people forget

Your body prioritizes keeping your core warm over your hands and feet, which is why fingers and toes go numb long before your torso feels cold. A warm hat, a buff or neck gaiter, insulated gloves with a wind-resistant outer, and wool hiking socks matter as much as your jacket choice — often more, since they're the first things to fail.

The detail that changes cold days most is a **spare thin glove liner**. Thick gloves have to come off for anything fiddly — a zip, a map, a snack wrapper — and bare hands in wind get useless fast. A thin liner underneath means you never go to bare skin, and the difference in how the day feels is out of all proportion to the weight.

Similarly, a buff is the highest-value item per gram in cold weather. It covers your neck, pulls up over your face in wind, works as a headband, and stops the draft down your collar that quietly makes everything else feel colder.

## Boots and socks decide the whole day

Cold feet ruin a hike faster than a cold torso, and they are usually caused by two things people get backwards. First, boots laced too tight, or a sock too thick for the boot, compress the space around your foot and cut circulation — a thick sock in a snug boot makes your feet colder, not warmer. Second, damp socks. Feet sweat all day, and by the afternoon a sock that started dry is not.

Carry one spare pair and change them at lunch. It takes two minutes and it resets the second half of the day.

## The overheating trap

The most common cold-weather mistake isn't underdressing — it's overdressing at the trailhead, then sweating through every layer within the first mile. Wet layers in cold weather are actively dangerous, because wet insulation stops insulating. Start cold on purpose, and adjust down before you start sweating, not after.

The practical version: strip a layer at the bottom of a climb, before you begin, not once you notice the sweat. And use the small controls first — open the front zip, push the sleeves up, take the hat off. Your head sheds heat quickly enough that a hat on and off works as a fine adjustment without stopping.

## Eat and drink more than you feel like

Two things go quietly wrong in the cold. Cold weather blunts thirst, so people drink far less than they need, and dehydration makes you feel colder and think worse. And staying warm burns energy — the body is running its own heating system — so the same walk costs you more calories than it would in summer.

If you are cold and adding layers is not helping, the answer is often food and water rather than another jacket. Keep snacks in a pocket you can reach without stopping, because anything requiring you to take the pack off in wind will not get eaten.

## Signs you need to turn back

Uncontrollable shivering, confusion, slurred speech, or fumbling hands are early hypothermia signs, not "toughing it out" territory. Add every dry layer you have, eat something with sugar for quick energy, and head back rather than pushing forward.

Two things worth knowing about this. It rarely feels dramatic from the inside — impaired judgement is one of the symptoms, which is exactly why the person affected is usually the last to suggest turning around. And it does not need freezing weather; a mild, wet, windy day with damp layers is a classic setup.

So watch the other people in your group rather than waiting to notice it in yourself, and agree before you set off that anyone can call the turnaround without having to argue for it.

## Quick reference

| Condition | Priority layer | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Clear, cold, calm | Mid layer for warmth | Overheating on climbs |
| Cold + windy | Shell over base | Wind chill on exposed sections |
| Cold + wet/snow | Waterproof shell, warm dry base | Wet insulation losing effectiveness |
| Cold + short daylight | Everything above, plus a headlamp | Being caught out after dark |

## What to carry that you hope not to wear

A cold-weather day pack should hold three things you may never touch: the puffy, the spare socks and gloves, and a foil blanket or bivvy bag. Together they weigh very little and they cover the situation this whole page is really about — being stationary, in the cold, for longer than you planned, because of an injury, a wrong turn, or waiting for someone.

That is the difference between an uncomfortable few hours and a genuine emergency, and it fits in a corner of the pack.

## Quick answers

**What should I wear hiking in cold weather?**
The full base-mid-shell system in heavier weights, plus real attention to hands, feet and head — extremities cool fastest once you stop moving.

**What should always be in a cold-weather day pack even if I never use it?**
A puffy insulated jacket, spare socks and gloves, and a foil blanket or bivvy bag — together they weigh very little and cover an unplanned stop from injury, a wrong turn, or waiting for someone.

**Why do cold-weather emergencies usually happen?**
Being stationary in the cold for longer than planned — an injury, a wrong turn or waiting for someone — turns a manageable hike into a real emergency far more often than the cold alone.

**How do I stay warm when I stop moving in cold weather?**
Put on a dedicated warm layer immediately, before you feel cold — your heat production drops sharply the moment you stop, and insulation only traps heat you still have.
