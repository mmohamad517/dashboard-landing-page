You can find north without a compass using a shadow stick, an analogue watch, or the night sky. None of them are precise. All of them are good enough to stop you walking in circles, which is usually the actual problem.

## The shadow stick method

The most reliable of the three, and it works anywhere on Earth.

1. Push a straight stick upright into open, level ground.
2. Mark the tip of its shadow with a stone.
3. Wait fifteen minutes — longer is better.
4. Mark the new shadow tip.

The line between the two marks runs roughly east–west. The first mark is west, the second is east, in either hemisphere. Stand with west on your left and you are facing north.

## The watch method

If you have an analogue watch set to true local time, point the hour hand at the sun. Halfway between the hour hand and twelve o'clock is roughly south in the northern hemisphere. In the southern hemisphere, point twelve at the sun instead; halfway between twelve and the hour hand is roughly north.

Be honest about its limits. Daylight saving throws it off by an hour. Near the equator it is close to useless, and around midday it is unreliable everywhere. Treat it as a sanity check on a direction you already suspect.

## The night sky

In the northern hemisphere, find the two stars at the end of the Plough's "pan" and follow them upward about five times their separation. That is Polaris, and it sits over true north. In the southern hemisphere, extend the long axis of the Southern Cross about four and a half times its own length, then drop straight down to the horizon for roughly south.

Night navigation in rough ground causes more injuries than it saves time. If you can wait for light, wait.

## Why "rough" is genuinely enough

People discard these methods because they are not precise. That misunderstands the problem. Without a reference, a walker in featureless ground drifts steadily to one side and comes back round on themselves — the circling that leaves people exhausted a short distance from where they started.

A direction that is roughly right breaks the circle. It does not need to be accurate to a degree; it needs to be consistent. Pick your north, pick something distant along your line, walk to it, then pick the next thing. That alone converts wandering into travelling.

## Natural signs, and why they are unreliable

You will read that moss grows on the north side of trees, that branches are denser to the south, or that snow melts first on southern slopes. There is a kernel of truth in each — sunlight is asymmetric — and each is wrong often enough to send you the wrong way.

Moss grows where it is damp and shaded, which depends far more on local shelter, prevailing wind and the lean of the tree than on compass direction. In a wood, adjacent trees will contradict each other.

Use them as a weak vote alongside a shadow stick or the sky, never as the answer. Where they are worth something is confirming a direction you already worked out by another method.

## The sun, roughly

The sun rises in the eastern half of the sky and sets in the western half, and around the middle of the day it is due south from the northern hemisphere and due north from the southern. The exact rising and setting points swing well north and south of east and west with the seasons, so this is a coarse check rather than a method — but coarse is enough to know whether you are heading broadly the wrong way.

## Keeping the line once you have it

Finding north is the easy half. Holding it while walking through trees, around obstacles and over broken ground is where people drift.

The technique is to navigate object to object. Pick something distinctive on your line as far ahead as you can see — a particular tree, a rock, a notch on a skyline — and walk to it without worrying about the ground between. When you reach it, pick the next one. If visibility is poor, send the strongest walker ahead to a visible point and call them onto the line before following.

Checking behind you matters as much: turn round every so often and look at where you came from, both to confirm you are still on a straight line and because the return journey looks completely different from the other direction.

## What matters more than any of this

Rough direction is enough to stop you circling, but the habit that actually prevents the problem happens before you leave: download an offline map, screenshot your route, and note the nearest road and the last junction you passed. A screenshot survives a dead data connection.

And if you are genuinely lost and someone knows your route — [staying put and signalling](/articles/what-to-do-if-you-get-lost-hiking/) beats navigating almost every time.
