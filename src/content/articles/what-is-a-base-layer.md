A base layer is the garment worn directly against your skin, and its job is not warmth. Its job is moisture: moving the sweat your body produces away from your skin so that you stay dry, because dry skin stays warm and wet skin does not.

That is the whole definition. Everything else — the fabrics, the fit, the weights, the price — is detail hanging off that one sentence.

## What a base layer actually does

Your body sweats whenever you work, including in the cold. If that sweat sits on your skin, it takes heat away from you as it evaporates, and it keeps doing that after you stop moving. A base layer's fabric pulls that moisture off the skin and spreads it out where it can evaporate, so you are not wearing a wet shirt when you stop for lunch on a cold ridge.

This is why the base layer is the piece that matters most in a layering system, even though it is the thinnest and the cheapest. Get it wrong and every layer over the top is working against a wet foundation.

**A base layer is not an insulating layer.** Warmth comes from the mid layer over the top, and protection from wind and rain comes from the shell over that. Confusing these jobs is the most common mistake in outdoor clothing — there is a [full breakdown of base versus mid](/articles/base-layer-vs-mid-layer-explained/) if that is the part you are stuck on.

## What "base layer" means in practice

The term covers any next-to-skin garment made from a fabric that manages moisture:

- **A base layer shirt** — long or short sleeve, worn under everything else
- **Base layer bottoms** — sometimes called long johns, thermal leggings or long underwear
- **Liner socks and glove liners** — the same principle applied to feet and hands

If someone says "base layer" with no other detail, they almost always mean the top.

## The fabrics, honestly compared

There are really three answers, and the right one depends on the trip rather than on which is "best".

### Merino wool

Fine wool from merino sheep, soft enough to wear against skin without itching. It moves moisture well, keeps some of its warmth when damp, and — the reason most people end up buying it — resists smelling for far longer than synthetics. That matters on any trip lasting more than a day.

The trade-offs are real: it costs more, it dries more slowly than synthetic, and thin merino wears out faster, particularly where a pack strap rubs.

### Synthetic (polyester, polypropylene)

Cheap, tough, and the fastest drying of the three. If you sweat heavily, or you are doing something short and hard and will wash the shirt afterwards, synthetic is genuinely the better choice.

The trade-off is odour. Synthetics hold onto smell in a way merino does not, and on a multi-day trip it becomes noticeable to you and to everyone with you.

### Blends

Merino mixed with a synthetic, aiming for merino's comfort and odour resistance with better durability and drying. For most people buying their first base layer, a blend is the sensible middle.

### And the one to avoid

**Cotton.** It absorbs moisture instead of moving it, holds it against your skin, loses all insulating value when wet, and takes a very long time to dry. This is where the outdoor saying "cotton kills" comes from, and while that is dramatic for a walk in the park, it is a fair description of what wet cotton does to you in cold, wet conditions.

Save the cotton t-shirt for the drive home.

## How a base layer should fit

**Snug, everywhere, without restricting you.** It has to touch your skin to move moisture off it — a loose base layer leaves air gaps and simply does not do its job.

Snug is not tight. You should be able to reach overhead and twist without the hem pulling out of your waistband. Long enough in the body to stay tucked, long enough in the sleeve to reach past your wrist when you raise your arm.

If you normally buy a size up for comfort, this is the one garment where you should not.

## Weights, and which one you need

Base layers come in different fabric weights, usually labelled lightweight, midweight and heavyweight, sometimes with a number for grams per square metre.

- **Lightweight** — warm weather, high effort, or under a lot of other layers
- **Midweight** — the most useful single choice for three-season use
- **Heavyweight** — genuinely cold conditions, or low-effort activity where you are not generating much heat

**If you are buying one and only one, buy midweight.** It covers the widest range of what most people actually do.

## When to wear one

The instinct is to think of base layers as winter clothing. They are not.

- **Cold weather:** obviously, as the foundation of the whole system.
- **Hot weather:** a light base layer moves sweat and provides sun cover, and is often more comfortable than bare skin under a pack.
- **Any trip where you will sweat and then stop.** That is the real trigger, and it applies in every season.

The one case where you do not need one is a short, easy outing close to help, in mild weather, where being briefly uncomfortable costs you nothing.

## Buying your first one

You do not need to spend a lot. A single midweight blend top in a plain colour will serve you across most of the year, and it is the piece of outdoor clothing that gives the most benefit per unit of money spent.

Buy one, wear it on a few trips, and let your own experience tell you whether you want merino for the smell or synthetic for the drying speed. Buying three at once before you know which problem bothers you more is how people end up with a drawer of unworn shirts.

## Washing, and the mistake that ruins them

Base layers are the item people quietly destroy in the laundry.

**Never use fabric softener.** It coats the fibres and stops them moving moisture, which removes the only thing the garment was bought to do. The shirt still looks fine and simply stops working.

Wash warm rather than hot, avoid the tumble dryer where you can, and wash merino gently on a wool setting. A base layer treated properly lasts years; one washed with softener is finished after a few cycles.

## The mistakes worth knowing about

**Wearing cotton underneath a good base layer.** It defeats the entire system. The base layer has to be the layer touching your skin.

**Buying it loose.** A base layer that is not in contact with skin is just a thin shirt.

**Treating a thick base layer as a mid layer.** A heavyweight base is still a base layer; it does not trap warm air the way a fleece does.

**Keeping it on to sleep in after a hard day.** If it is damp, it will make you cold all night. Change into a dry set — see [what to wear for cold-weather camping](/articles/what-to-wear-for-cold-weather-camping/).

## Quick answers

**What is a base layer used for?**
Moving sweat off your skin so you stay dry. Warmth is the mid layer's job.

**What is a base layer shirt?**
A close-fitting top made from merino, synthetic or a blend, worn directly against the skin under everything else.

**What is the difference between a base layer and a mid layer?**
The base layer manages moisture; the mid layer traps warm air. Base goes against the skin, mid goes over it, shell goes over both.

**Is a base layer the same as thermal underwear?**
Broadly yes. "Thermals" is the older everyday word for the same garments, though modern base layers are designed around moving moisture rather than warmth alone.

**Can I use a normal t-shirt as a base layer?**
Only if it is not cotton. A synthetic sports top will work; a cotton t-shirt will hold sweat against you and make you colder.

**Do I need a base layer in summer?**
Often yes. A lightweight one manages sweat and covers your skin from the sun, and both matter on a long day.

**How many base layers should I own?**
One good midweight top covers most trips. A second lets you keep one permanently dry for sleeping on overnight trips.

## The one-line version

**A base layer keeps you dry, not warm — so buy it snug, in anything but cotton, and never wash it with fabric softener.**
