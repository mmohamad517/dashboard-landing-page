Give a child one real job, and they will own it. Give them a safety briefing, and they will nod through it. The difference is whether the outcome visibly depends on them.

## One job, and it is genuinely theirs

Not a token task. Something the camp actually needs: the tent pegs, the water, checking the fire is properly out, the head torch inventory. If it does not get done, it is noticeably not done.

Rotate jobs between trips so they collect skills rather than a specialism.

The hard part is not assigning the job. It is leaving it alone. A tent pitched slightly badly by a nine-year-old teaches more than a tent pitched perfectly by an adult who took over halfway through, and children can tell instantly when a job is real and when it is being quietly redone behind them. Step in for safety. Otherwise let it stand.

## Roughly by age

**Small.** Carrying their own small pack, collecting kindling, staying within a set boundary, knowing the whistle rule.

**Middle.** Pitching a tent with help, using a whistle to signal, filtering and boiling water, laying a fire that an adult lights, reading a simple map.

**Older.** Route planning, cooking a meal for the group, navigating a leg, running the shelter build, being the one who knows the emergency plan.

These are rough bands, not a schedule. Match the job to the individual child's confidence and attention rather than their birthday — some eight-year-olds are ready for a map, and some twelve-year-olds are not yet ready to be trusted near a stove.

## The rules that never bend

- **The whistle rule.** Everyone carries one. Three blasts means "I need you", and it is always answered without annoyance, every single time — including false alarms. The moment a child fears being told off for using it, the whistle is useless.
- **Never anything wild goes in a mouth.** Not berries, not mushrooms, not leaves. No exceptions, no clever tests, no "unless an adult says". This is the rule that should be absolutely rigid.
- **Fire has a keeper.** One named adult, present.
- **Nobody walks alone.**

One more worth adding as they get older: **if you are lost, stop and stay put.** A child who keeps walking is far harder to find than one sitting under a tree blowing a whistle. Practise it as a drill, not a warning — "what do you do if you look up and can't see us?" — until the answer is automatic.

## Let them be cold once, briefly, safely

A child who has been slightly cold because they left the fleece in the tent understands layering in a way no explanation achieves. Somewhere safe, briefly, with the fix right there.

The same principle covers most of it: let the small mistake happen where it is harmless.

## Teach the decision, not just the skill

Pitching a tent is a skill. Choosing where to pitch it is a decision — not in the dry riverbed, not under the dead branch, not at the bottom of the cold hollow. Decisions transfer to situations you have not trained for. Skills do not.

The way to teach a decision is to ask rather than tell. "Where should we put the tent, and why not over there?" takes a minute longer than pointing at a spot, and it is the difference between a child who can pitch a tent and a child who can choose a campsite.

## Practise at home

Pitch the tent in the garden. Cook one camp meal in the kitchen. Do the seven-day skills challenge in the week before the trip. Everything is easier the second time, and the first time should not be in the dark in the rain.

This is also where the kit gets sorted out cheaply. Boots that rub, a sleeping bag that turns out to be too thin, a head torch with dead batteries — all much better discovered in the garden.

## Keeping the first trips short

The most common way to put a child off camping is a first trip that is too long, too far, or too ambitious. One night close to home, with an easy exit if it goes badly, does more for the next five years of trips than a demanding weekend.

Warmth, food and sleep decide whether a child enjoys camping. Almost nothing else does. A child who was warm, well fed and slept reasonably will want to come again, regardless of the scenery.

## Handling the moments it goes wrong

Something will. A child will be tired, or frightened by a noise, or in tears about a blister at the worst possible point of the walk.

The response that works is to stop and deal with it properly rather than pressing on. Food, a warm layer, ten minutes sitting down, and taking the complaint seriously will fix most of it. What does not work is treating discomfort as something to be pushed through — children learn from that trip that outdoors means being ignored while miserable, and that lesson sticks harder than any skill.

## What you are actually teaching

The specific skills matter less than three habits: noticing conditions before they become problems, telling someone when something is wrong, and being willing to attempt something you might do badly.

Those three transfer to every situation a child will meet outdoors, including the ones nobody planned for.

## Quick answers

**How do I teach kids camping skills?**
Give them one real, needed job per trip rather than a lecture — a task the camp actually depends on teaches far more than being told what to do.

**What age should kids start learning camping skills?**
Match the task to the individual child's confidence rather than a fixed age — younger kids can carry a small pack or collect kindling, older kids can navigate a leg or plan a meal.

**What is the most important camping safety rule for kids?**
Never anything wild goes in a mouth — no berries, mushrooms or leaves, with no exceptions. This is the one rule that should stay completely rigid.

**How do I make camping skills stick for kids?**
Let small, safe mistakes happen rather than preventing every one — a child who was briefly cold because they left a layer behind remembers the lesson far better than being told about it.
