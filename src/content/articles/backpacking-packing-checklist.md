Overnight backpacking adds three categories to a day hike list — shelter, a sleep system, and cooking gear — and changes the clothing question from "what do I wear for a few hours" to "what do I wear through a full day and a cold night," which is where most beginners either overpack or get genuinely cold.

## Clothing for an overnight trip

Bring one hiking outfit (worn) plus one dedicated, dry sleep outfit that never gets worn during the day — this matters more than most gear lists admit, because damp hiking clothes in a sleeping bag will keep you cold all night. Layer as you would for a day hike, but add a warmer mid layer than you'd otherwise carry, since camp temperatures after sunset drop faster than most people expect.

## Shelter and sleep system

- Tent or shelter, appropriate for the season and expected wind
- Sleeping bag rated at or below the coldest temperature you expect, not the average
- Sleeping pad — as much for insulation from the ground as for comfort
- A dry stuff sack for your sleep clothes specifically

## Cooking and water

A single small stove, one pot, and a lighter (plus backup matches) cover almost every simple backpacking dinner. Bring a water filter or purification tablets rather than carrying all your water — it's lighter and more reliable over a full trip.

## The weight tradeoff beginners get wrong

New backpackers tend to pack for every possible scenario, which produces a bag heavy enough to make the trip miserable and end it early. Every "just in case" item should answer a real question: what specific situation, given your actual route and forecast, does this solve? If you can't answer that, it stays home.

## A realistic overnight list

| Category | Bring | Skip |
|---|---|---|
| Clothing | 1 hiking set + 1 dry sleep set | A full spare outfit "just because" |
| Shelter | Season-appropriate tent | Oversized car-camping tent |
| Cooking | 1 stove, 1 pot | Multi-pot cook sets for one person |
