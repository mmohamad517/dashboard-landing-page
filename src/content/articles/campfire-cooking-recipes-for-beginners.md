The one thing that separates good camp food from bad: cook on coals, not flames. Flames burn the outside and leave the middle raw. Let the fire burn down, rake the embers flat, and cook over that.

## Foil packet dinner

Sliced sausage or halloumi, potato, onion, a splash of oil, seasoning. Slice everything thin and roughly the same size so it cooks evenly. Wrap tightly in doubled foil, sit it on the coals, turn once halfway. About half an hour.

No washing up — you eat out of the foil.

## One-pot pasta

Small pasta shapes in just enough water to cover. Let it absorb rather than draining it — you keep the starch, which thickens the sauce. Stir in tomato paste and hard cheese off the heat. Twelve minutes, one pot.

## One-pan shakshuka

Soften onion and pepper, add tinned tomatoes and paprika, simmer, make wells and crack eggs in, cover until set. Looks far more impressive than the effort involved.

## Bag omelette

Eggs, cheese and whatever vegetables you have, mixed in a sealed freezer bag with the air pressed out, dropped into boiling water. Roll it straight onto a plate. Zero cleanup, and everyone can have their own combination.

## Twist bread

Flour, water, salt, baking powder, mixed to a stiff dough in a bag. Wind a rope of it around a green stick and turn it slowly over embers. Done when it slides off cleanly.

## Banana boats

Split a banana in its skin, push chocolate into the split, wrap in foil, edge of the coals for ten minutes. Eaten with a spoon.

## Reading the coals

You do not need a thermometer, just your hand held about a palm's width above the embers. If you have to pull it away almost immediately, that is a hot bed — good for searing and for foil packets you will turn often. If you can hold it there for a slow count of several seconds, that is a moderate bed — the right one for most of these recipes. Longer than that and you are cooking on a fire that is dying; rake it together or add a little wood and wait.

Build the fire to one side and rake embers across as you need them. That gives you a hot zone and a cool zone, which is the campfire version of turning the heat down — and it is the single technique that stops beginners burning dinner.

## What to prep before you leave home

Camp cooking is easy or miserable depending on what you did in your kitchen:

- **Chop the vegetables** and bag them. Dicing an onion by head torch is nobody's idea of a holiday.
- **Pre-mix dry ingredients** — the bread mix, the spice blend — so at camp it is just add water.
- **Portion by meal, not by ingredient.** One bag per dinner means you never hunt through a crate.
- **Freeze one meal.** It thaws over the first day and doubles as an ice pack in the cool box.

## A first-trip menu that works

Friday, arriving late and possibly wet: something that needs no cooking at all. Bread, cheese, cured meat, fruit. Do not plan a first fire for the night you arrive tired in the dark.

Saturday breakfast: bag omelette, or bread and coffee. Saturday dinner: foil packets, because they are forgiving and there is no washing up. Sunday breakfast: twist bread over the last of the embers, which is the meal children remember.

That is four meals, one pot, and almost no cleanup — and it leaves you time to actually sit by the fire.

## Fire safety, briefly

Fire rules change by region and season, and open fires are banned outright in many places for part of the year. Check before you plan an evening around one.

When you do have a fire: keep it small, keep water or soil within arm's reach, never leave it unattended, and put it out properly before bed — soak, stir, soak again, and check the ashes are cold with the back of your hand. Cooking involves hot metal and boiling water in the dark, so keep the pot handle turned inward and away from where people walk.

## The practical bits

- **Wide, shallow pot with a lid** boils faster and uses less fuel than a deep narrow one.
- **Prep at home.** Chop, mix dry ingredients, pre-portion. Doing it in the dark with cold hands is miserable.
- **One large zip bag** for all dry food — it opens flat as a clean surface, and it is one thing to hang away from animals.
- **Two sticks** work as tongs for a hot pot. Practise on a cold one; a spilled pot of boiling water is a common camp burn.

## Quick answers

**What is easy campfire cooking for beginners?**
One-pot meals and foil-wrapped food, prepped at home so the only work left at camp is heating and combining — chopping and measuring in the dark with cold hands is the part beginners struggle with.

**What pot is best for campfire cooking?**
A wide, shallow pot with a lid. It boils faster and uses less fuel than a deep, narrow one for the same amount of food.

**How do I prep camp meals in advance?**
Chop, mix dry ingredients, and pre-portion everything at home, then store it in one large zip bag per meal — flat, easy to hang away from animals, and quick to open as a clean surface.

**What is a common campfire cooking mistake?**
Handling a hot pot without proper tongs. Two sticks work, but practise on a cold pot first — a spilled pot of boiling water is one of the most common camp burns.
