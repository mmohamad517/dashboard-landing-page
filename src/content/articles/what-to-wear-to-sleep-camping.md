The short answer is: not whatever you hiked or sat around the fire in. A dedicated sleep set — clothes that only ever go on inside the bag — is one of the cheapest, lightest things you can carry, and it's the single most common thing beginners skip until a cold night teaches them why it matters.

## Why your day clothes don't belong in the bag

Anything worn during the day has picked up sweat, even on a mild day and even if it doesn't feel damp. A sleeping bag works by trapping the warm air your body produces — it doesn't generate heat on its own. Damp clothing inside it cools you steadily overnight instead, the same way a wet base layer works against you while hiking. A sleep set exists to guarantee that what's touching your skin overnight is genuinely dry, not just dry-feeling.

## The base sleep outfit, any season

- **A dry base layer top and bottoms** — the same moisture-managing principle as [daytime base layers](/articles/what-to-wear-for-cold-weather-camping/), just a set that's never worn hiking
- **Dry socks**, kept sealed until you're in the bag
- **A hat**, since a surprising amount of heat is lost from an uncovered head overnight

That's the whole list for most three-season conditions. It doesn't need to be technical or expensive — the only requirement is that it stays completely dry and completely separate from anything worn during the day.

## Adjusting for the season

**Warm weather:** Often just the base layer, or even less if the night genuinely stays warm — the goal is comfort, not maximum coverage. A liner-only sleeping bag or just a light sheet inside it is common in real heat.

**Shoulder season:** The base sleep outfit above, plus a light hat and socks, covers most nights that aren't genuinely cold.

**Cold weather:** This is where the sleep system needs real thought, not just an extra layer thrown on — [what to wear for cold-weather camping](/articles/what-to-wear-for-cold-weather-camping/) covers the full breakdown, including why a separate camp-only insulated layer and proper ground insulation matter as much as what you're wearing inside the bag itself.

## What not to wear to sleep

**Cotton**, for the same reason it's avoided all day — it holds moisture rather than moving it, and a slightly damp cotton shirt at 2am is a genuinely cold few hours.

**Too many layers stuffed into a mummy bag.** A sleeping bag insulates with loft — the air trapped in the fill — and bulky clothing compressed against the bag's inner wall can flatten that loft exactly where your body needs it most. If you're cold in a bag that should be warm enough, the fix is often a liner or a warmer bag, not another layer of clothing squeezed inside it.

**Anything worn during the day "because it's basically dry."** The rule that makes a sleep set work is that it's never in question — the moment it's worn around camp for an hour, it stops being the dry layer you're counting on at 2am.

## Keep it in its own dry bag

Pack the sleep set in its own stuff sack, sealed, separate from daytime clothing — the same habit covered in the [backpacking checklist](/articles/backpacking-packing-checklist/). This does two things: it keeps the set dry even if the rest of the pack gets wet, and it removes the temptation to dig through it and "just borrow" a piece during the day.

## If you get cold in the night

Adding the warm layer you were wearing at camp — as long as it's dry — is a reasonable fix for one cold night, better than staying cold and telling yourself it'll pass. But treat it as a sign to reassess for the next night: a sleep set that isn't warm enough on a trip is worth fixing properly rather than patching with daytime layers each time.

## Quick answers

**What should I wear to sleep in a sleeping bag while camping?**
A dry base layer top and bottoms, dry socks, and a hat for most three-season conditions — clothes that are never worn during the day, kept sealed until you're in the bag.

**Can I sleep in the clothes I hiked in?**
Not if you want to stay warm. Even clothing that feels only slightly damp noticeably reduces how warm a sleeping bag keeps you overnight.

**Should I wear more clothes if I'm cold at night in my sleeping bag?**
A little, if it's genuinely dry — but bulky clothing can compress a mummy bag's insulation and make it less effective. If you're regularly cold, a liner or a warmer-rated bag usually solves it better than adding more layers.

**Do I need different sleep clothes for summer versus winter camping?**
Yes — a light base layer often covers warm nights, while cold-weather camping needs a fuller system including ground insulation and a dedicated camp-only warm layer, covered in the [cold-weather camping guide](/articles/what-to-wear-for-cold-weather-camping/).
