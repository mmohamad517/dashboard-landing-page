The best outdoor layering fabrics share one property depending on the layer: base layers need to wick moisture, mid layers need to trap air without holding water, and shells need to block wind and water while still letting vapor escape — and cotton fails at nearly all three, which is why it's the one fabric outdoor guides consistently warn against.

## Base layer fabrics

**Merino wool** wicks moisture, resists odor, and insulates even when slightly damp — the most forgiving base layer fabric, at a higher price point. **Synthetic wicking fabrics** (polyester, nylon blends) perform similarly for moisture management at a lower cost, though they hold odor more over multi-day trips. Both outperform cotton by a wide margin for this specific job.

The trade-offs between the two are practical rather than technical. Merino is softer against skin, stays wearable for days without becoming unpleasant, and costs more. It is also more delicate — thin merino wears through at pack-strap contact points faster than synthetic does. Synthetic is cheap, tough, dries faster, and starts to smell after a day of hard work, which matters on a multi-day trip and not at all on a day hike.

Blends of the two exist and are a reasonable compromise: most of the odour resistance, better durability, lower price.

## Mid layer fabrics

**Fleece** is affordable, dries fast, and insulates reasonably well even damp — a solid default mid layer. **Synthetic insulation** (like polyester fill jackets) keeps insulating even if it gets wet, making it more forgiving than down in unpredictable weather. **Down** offers the best warmth-to-weight ratio but loses almost all insulating power when wet, so it's a better choice for cold, dry conditions than damp or rainy ones.

Fleece comes in weights, usually labelled with a number — lower numbers are thinner and more breathable, higher numbers warmer and bulkier. A thin fleece you will actually keep on while walking is more useful than a thick one you have to remove at the first climb.

The honest summary: fleece for value and reliability, synthetic fill for wet climates, down for cold and dry conditions where pack space is tight.

## Shell fabrics

**Waterproof-breathable membranes** (the fabric behind most "technical" rain jackets) block water while venting some internal moisture — better for sustained rain. **Wind-resistant, non-waterproof shells** breathe more and pack smaller — better for wind and light weather where sustained rain isn't the main risk. Neither is strictly "better"; they solve different problems.

There is a detail here that explains most complaints about expensive rain jackets. The waterproof membrane is on the inside; the outer face fabric is treated with a water-repellent finish so rain beads and rolls off. That finish wears off with use and washing. Once it does, the face fabric soaks through and the membrane can no longer pass vapour outward, so you get damp inside and conclude the jacket has failed. It usually hasn't — washing it and re-applying a proofing treatment restores the behaviour, and costs a fraction of a new jacket.

## Softshell: the in-between category

Softshell fabric sits between a fleece and a hard shell: stretchy, breathable, wind-resistant and water-repellent rather than waterproof. It is genuinely comfortable for cold, dry, windy conditions and for high-effort walking where a waterproof would leave you soaked from the inside.

Its limitation is that it is not a rain jacket, and treating it as one is a common and expensive mistake. In sustained rain it wets out. For most people a softshell is a nice-to-have third option, not a replacement for a waterproof.

## Where cotton is genuinely fine

To be fair to cotton: it is comfortable, cheap, hard-wearing, and perfectly reasonable in the right place. A cotton t-shirt on a warm dry walk close to the car is fine. Cotton is also a good choice for what you sleep in at camp, where you are not sweating and you want comfort. Heavy cotton canvas has a long history as tough outer wear in dry climates.

The advice to avoid it is about a specific combination: cotton, next to skin, in cold or wet conditions, far from help. Knowing why the rule exists tells you when it does not apply.

## Why cotton fails specifically

Cotton fibers absorb water rather than moving it along, so a cotton layer holds onto sweat or rain and stays wet for hours. Wet cotton against your skin pulls heat away from your body significantly faster than dry fabric — fine on a warm, dry day, and genuinely risky in cold or wet conditions, where it's a documented contributor to hypothermia even at temperatures well above freezing.

The compounding problem is drying time. A synthetic base layer that gets soaked can dry against your body while you keep walking. A cotton one cannot, so a single soaking early in the day stays with you until you change or the day ends.

## Reading a label without being sold to

Fabric marketing uses a lot of proprietary names for what are ultimately a few material families. Turn the garment inside out and read the composition tag instead. Polyester, nylon, polypropylene and merino wool in various proportions cover almost everything worth wearing next to skin. If the tag says a high percentage of cotton, you know what you are getting regardless of what the hang tag claims.

For shells, the numbers manufacturers quote for waterproofness and breathability are measured in laboratory tests that differ between brands, so they are useful for comparing two jackets from the same maker and much less useful across brands. Fit, venting and hood design affect your real-world comfort more than a rating on a tag.

## Fabric care, briefly

- **Base layers**: wash more often than they smell like they need it — oils clog the fibres that wick. Never use fabric softener on technical fabrics.
- **Fleece**: keep it away from high dryer heat, which damages the pile.
- **Down**: wash rarely, with a down-specific detergent, and dry thoroughly to restore loft. A damp down jacket packed away is a ruined down jacket.
- **Waterproof shells**: wash them when water stops beading, then re-proof. A dirty shell is a less breathable shell.

## A simple fabric rule

If a layer's job is to be worn directly against your skin or to keep you warm when wet, avoid cotton for it. For outer layers where waterproofing is doing the work (rather than the fabric's inherent properties), the rule matters less.

And if you are choosing between spending money on a better fabric or spending it on a layer you do not own at all, buy the missing layer. A cheap wind shell you have with you beats an excellent one you decided to save up for.

## Quick answers

**What is the best fabric for outdoor layering?**
Merino wool or synthetic for anything against the skin or holding warmth, chosen based on whether odour resistance (merino) or fast drying and low cost (synthetic) matters more for the trip.

**Why is cotton bad for hiking?**
It absorbs moisture instead of moving it away from your skin, loses nearly all insulating value once wet, and dries very slowly — a real risk in cold, wet conditions.

**Is merino wool better than synthetic for hiking?**
Neither is universally better — merino resists odour longer and stays warmer when damp, synthetic dries faster and costs less. The right choice depends on trip length and conditions.

**Does the fabric rule apply to shell jackets too?**
Less so — a shell's waterproofing comes from its coating or membrane, not the base fabric, so the cotton rule matters far more for base and mid layers than for outer shells.
