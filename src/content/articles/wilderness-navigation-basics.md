Navigation is mostly about staying found. Every technique for working out where you are is a recovery from a failure that better habits would have prevented.

## Know where you are, continuously

The single habit that separates people who get lost from people who do not: keep a rough running idea of where you are. Not precise — approximate, and updated.

Every twenty minutes or so: how long have I walked, roughly how fast, roughly which direction, what have I passed? That is enough to keep you within a manageable area if something goes wrong.

## Before you leave

- Download offline maps for the whole area, not just your route.
- Screenshot the route and the access points. A screenshot survives a dead data connection; a browser tab does not.
- Note the nearest road, and the last junction before the trailhead.
- Tell one person the route and your return time.

That last item is navigation equipment. It determines how fast anyone finds you.

## Handrails and catching features

Two ideas that make navigation dramatically easier:

A **handrail** is a long feature you follow — a river, a ridge, a wall, a track. Following one is far more robust than holding a bearing across open ground.

A **catching feature** is something you cannot miss that tells you you have gone too far — a road, a river, a change in the ground. Knowing yours means an error becomes "I have overshot" instead of "I am lost".

## Aiming off

If you are heading for a bridge on a river, deliberately aim to one side rather than straight at it. Hit the river, and you know which way to turn. Aim straight and miss, and you have a coin flip.

## Compass, briefly

Learn to take a bearing off a map and follow it, and to orient the map to the ground. That covers most of what a walker needs. The rest is worth learning but rarely decisive.

Practise where you already know the answer.

## Phone and paper

Use the phone — offline maps and GPS are genuinely excellent and there is no virtue in refusing them. But carry a compass and know roughly where you are without looking, because batteries die, screens crack, and cold flattens phones fast.

## Pacing and timing

Two ways to know how far you have come without any instrument.

**Timing** is the simpler one: note the time you leave a known point. Most walkers cover ground at a fairly consistent rate on similar terrain, and climbing costs far more time than flat distance. After a few trips you will know your own pace well enough to estimate usefully.

**Pacing** is counting double-steps over a known distance so you know roughly how many of yours make a hundred metres. It sounds fussy and it is genuinely useful in poor visibility, where a hundred metres and four hundred look identical.

Neither needs to be exact. Both turn "somewhere along this path" into "about here", which is the difference between a small search area and a large one.

## Contours, briefly

The single most useful thing a map tells you is the shape of the ground, and most beginners look straight past it.

Lines close together mean steep, lines far apart mean gentle. A V pointing uphill is a valley or stream; a V or U pointing downhill is a ridge or spur. Concentric rings are a summit.

That is enough to answer the questions that matter on the day: is this climb going to be brutal, is there a gentler line, and does the shape of the ground in front of me match the shape on the map? That last check is how you confirm where you are without any instrument at all.

## Navigating in poor visibility

Mist, dusk and heavy rain remove the distant features everything above depends on, and this is when people get into trouble on ground they know.

What still works: handrails, catching features, aiming off, pacing and timing. What stops working: picking a distant object, judging distance by eye, and recognising terrain.

So in poor visibility, shorten everything. Navigate leg by leg between features you are confident of, keep the group together and in sight, and slow down. If the safest option is to stop and wait for the cloud to lift or for light, that is a decision rather than a failure.

## Practise where the answer is known

The worst time to learn navigation is when you need it. Take a map and compass to somewhere you know well and use them anyway — orient the map, take a bearing, follow it, check the contours against what you can see.

It takes one afternoon, it costs nothing, and it converts equipment you are carrying into a skill you have.

## When it goes wrong

Stop walking. Sit down. [The first ten minutes matter more than the next two hours](/articles/what-to-do-if-you-get-lost-hiking/).
