Start with the honest part: reading an article does not make you capable in an emergency. This is a map of what you will most likely face and where the hard limits are. If you take one action after reading it, book a first aid course.

A hands-on course teaches this properly, with practice and correction, in a way no article can. A wilderness-specific one adds the part that matters most outdoors: what to do when help is hours away rather than minutes.

## Before anything else

Two habits do more than any technique in this article.

**Check the scene is safe before you approach.** Whatever injured them can injure you, and a second casualty makes everything worse. Unstable ground, falling rock, water, traffic, fire — look before you move.

**Work out early whether you need help coming.** Getting a call out takes time, and mountain rescue or emergency services would far rather be stood down than called late. Deciding early is not overreacting.

## Bleeding

Press hard, directly on the wound, with the cleanest cloth you have. Keep pressing — five minutes without lifting to peek. Raise the limb if you can. If blood soaks through, add more cloth on top rather than removing the first layer.

Wear gloves if you have them. Once bleeding is controlled, keep the person warm — blood loss and cold together are considerably worse than either alone.

**Stop and call for help** if bleeding is spurting, will not stop, or keeps soaking through.

## Burns

Cool under running water for twenty minutes. Not ice — ice damages tissue. Remove rings and watches early, before swelling starts. Cover loosely with a clean, non-fluffy cloth, and keep the rest of the person warm, because cooling a burn cools the whole person.

No butter, no ointment, no bursting blisters.

Camp burns come overwhelmingly from stoves, boiling water and fires, and most of them are preventable: a stable stove base, long sleeves when handling pots, and no fuel near a lit flame.

**Call for help** for any burn bigger than the person's palm, or any burn on the face, hands or joints. Also for any burn that goes all the way through the skin, any electrical burn, and any burn to a child.

## Sprains

Stop. Do not walk it off. Cold pack wrapped in cloth for twenty minutes, support firmly but not tightly, and raise it when resting. A trekking pole makes a usable crutch for a short walk out.

Check that fingers or toes beyond any support stay warm and their normal colour. If they do not, the support is too tight — loosen it.

**Call for help** if they cannot bear weight, or the joint looks deformed.

## Blisters

Not an emergency, and the single most common thing that ends a trip early.

Deal with a hot spot the moment you feel it, not at the next scheduled stop. Stopping to tape a warm patch of skin takes two minutes; a full blister costs you the rest of the walk. Keep the skin over an intact blister rather than opening it — it is the best dressing available — and pad around it so pressure goes anywhere but the blister itself.

## Head injuries

Any blow to the head is worth taking seriously outdoors, because you are a long way from assessment.

**Get help immediately** for anyone who lost consciousness even briefly, is confused or repeating themselves, is vomiting, has a worsening headache, or is behaving unlike themselves. Do not let anyone who has taken a knock to the head continue alone, and do not let them sleep unobserved.

## Overheating

Shade immediately, loosen clothing, wet the skin and fan them. Sips of cool water if they are fully alert.

**This is an emergency** if there is confusion, collapse, or they have stopped sweating in the heat. Call, and keep cooling while you wait.

## Getting cold

Out of the wind, off the ground, dry layers on — even over wet ones. Warm sweet drink if fully alert. Insulate underneath, not just over.

**This is an emergency** if shivering stops while they are still cold, or they become confused or clumsy. Handle them gently and call.

## Allergic reactions

Ask about allergies before the trip, not during one. If someone carries an adrenaline auto-injector, know where it is and that they are willing for you to help.

**A severe allergic reaction is an immediate emergency**: difficulty breathing, swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, or sudden collapse. Call emergency services straight away, and use the person's own auto-injector according to its instructions if they have one and cannot use it themselves.

## What to carry

A basic kit with plenty of dressings and tape, blister care, gloves, and any personal medication the group needs. Add a foil blanket and a whistle. The kit matters far less than knowing what to do with it.

Two additions worth making: enough dressings to actually control a real bleed rather than the token few that come in most pre-made kits, and a small waterproof card listing the group's allergies, medications and emergency contacts.

Check the kit before each trip. Tape perishes, plasters lose their stick, and medication expires — a kit last opened two years ago is not a kit.

## When you call for help

Say it in this order, because it is the order they need it in:

1. **Where you are** — grid reference, coordinates from your phone, or the clearest description you can give of the route and nearest landmark.
2. **What has happened**, and how many people are hurt.
3. **The person's condition**: are they alert, are they breathing normally, what are the injuries.
4. **Your phone battery and signal**, and the group's number, kit and shelter.

Then stay where you are if you safely can. Moving after making the call is one of the most common ways a rescue gets complicated.

## The pattern worth memorising

Across nearly every scenario above, the same two signs mean *stop improvising and call*: **confusion**, and **anything that is getting worse rather than better**.
