In cold conditions, the thing that gets people is not usually the temperature. It is being wet — from weather or from their own sweat — and staying still.

## Manage sweat before you manage cold

Wet insulation stops insulating, and it is slow to dry in the field. Someone who sweats through their layers on a climb and then stops moving at the top can go from comfortable to dangerously cold in minutes.

Start slightly cold. Vent or shed a layer *before* you are sweating, not after. Put the warm layer back on the moment you stop.

The instinct runs the other way, which is why this needs stating so bluntly. Standing at the trailhead in the cold, every part of you wants to put on more clothing. Twenty minutes later, working hard uphill, that clothing is doing active harm. The discipline is to be slightly uncomfortable at the start in exchange for being dry at the top.

## The four ways you lose heat

Knowing these turns cold-weather decisions from guesswork into something you can reason about.

**Evaporation.** Sweat, and moisture in your breath. This is the one people cause themselves, and it is the largest avoidable loss.

**Conduction.** Direct contact with something cold — the ground, a rock you sat on, wet clothing against skin. Water conducts heat away from you far faster than air does, which is the whole reason wet clothing is so dangerous.

**Convection.** Moving air carrying away the warm layer your body has built up around itself. This is what a shell stops, and why wind changes everything about how cold a given temperature feels.

**Radiation.** Straightforward heat loss to colder surroundings, largest from any skin left uncovered.

Every piece of cold-weather advice worth following is addressing one of those four. When you are unsure what to do, work out which one is happening to you.

## The layers, briefly

- **Base:** moves sweat off your skin. Never cotton.
- **Mid:** traps warm air. On at stops, off while working hard.
- **Shell:** blocks wind and rain, lets moisture out.

Wind stripping heat away matters as much as air temperature.

## Extremities

Your body protects your core first, so fingers and toes go before your torso feels cold. A warm hat, gloves with a spare thin liner pair, and dry wool socks do more for comfort than a heavier jacket.

Wet feet in freezing conditions are an injury risk, not just discomfort.

Boots laced too tightly, or socks doubled up until they compress your feet, make this worse rather than better — restricting circulation to your feet in the cold is counterproductive. Warmth in extremities comes from blood reaching them, so anything tight enough to reduce that is working against you.

## Eating and drinking in the cold

Two things get overlooked in the cold because neither feels urgent.

**You dehydrate in cold weather** and you do not notice, because the thirst signal is weaker and you can see the moisture leaving as breath rather than feeling it as sweat. Dehydration makes you less able to keep warm. Drink on a schedule rather than waiting to feel thirsty, and keep water where it will not freeze — insulated, close to your body, or bottles stored upside down so ice forms away from the cap.

**You need more food than usual**, because keeping warm has a real energy cost. Someone who has not eaten will get cold faster than someone who has, regardless of clothing. A hot drink and something to eat is a legitimate response to feeling cold, not a comfort measure.

## Hypothermia: the early signs

This is the part to memorise, because it is easier to spot in someone else than in yourself:

- Shivering they cannot control
- Fumbling with zips and buckles
- Going quiet, or stumbling
- Poor decisions, slurred words

**Shivering that stops while they are still cold is not improvement.** It is a serious sign. So is confusion. Both mean call for help.

The reason this must be memorised rather than looked up is that the person affected loses the judgement needed to recognise it in themselves. The affected person will often insist they are fine. In a group, everyone should know the signs, and anyone should be able to call a stop.

## What to do

Out of the wind, off the ground. Swap wet layers for dry, or add dry layers over the top. Warm sweet drink if they are fully alert. Insulate underneath — the ground is taking heat the whole time. Handle them gently.

A few specifics that matter:

- **Off the ground is not optional.** Sleeping mats, packs, spare clothing, branches — anything between the person and the cold ground. Insulating underneath does more than piling clothing on top.
- **Only give drinks to someone who is fully alert** and able to hold and swallow them without difficulty.
- **No alcohol.** It feels warming and does the opposite.
- **Handle them gently and keep them horizontal** rather than walking them about.
- **Someone who has stopped shivering, is confused, or is not fully alert needs emergency medical help.** That is beyond what field care can fix, and getting help moving early matters more than anything you do at the scene.

None of this replaces proper first-aid training. If you spend time outdoors in the cold, a wilderness first-aid course is the single most useful thing you can do, and it teaches this properly with hands-on practice.

## Sleeping

A sleeping bag rated for the coldest night you expect, not the average. Change into dry clothes reserved for sleeping, even if the day's layers feel only slightly damp. Insulate under the bag thickly.

Go to bed warm rather than expecting the bag to warm you — a bag traps the heat you bring into it and does not generate any of its own. Something to eat before sleeping, and a few minutes of gentle movement to warm up first, both make a noticeable difference. Keep tomorrow's socks and any batteries inside the bag with you.

## The habits that matter most

1. Start cold, adjust early, never sweat through your layers.
2. Get off the ground and out of the wind whenever you stop.
3. Eat and drink before you feel you need to.
4. Know the hypothermia signs well enough to spot them in someone else.
5. Turn back earlier than feels necessary. Cold problems compound.
