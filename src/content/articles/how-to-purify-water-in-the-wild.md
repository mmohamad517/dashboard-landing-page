Boiling is the method that works everywhere, needs no batteries or spare parts, and handles every biological threat. If you can boil, boil.

## Boiling

Bring the water to a rolling boil. That is the point at which it is safe — the "how many minutes" arguments matter far less than actually reaching a proper boil. At high altitude, hold it a little longer.

Boiling does nothing about chemical contamination or heavy metals, which is why you still choose your source sensibly: flowing water above human activity beats a stagnant pool below a farm.

## Filtering, and what it does not do

Pouring water through a bandana, coffee filter or clean sock removes grit, leaf matter and sediment. It does not remove the things that make you ill. Cloth filtering is a preparation step, not a treatment.

A proper backpacking filter is different — a real filter with a rated pore size removes bacteria and protozoa. Most do not remove viruses, which matters more in some regions than others.

## Improvised sediment filter

If your only water is thick with silt, cut the base off a bottle, invert it, and layer cloth, then charcoal from a cold campfire, then sand, then gravel. Pour slowly.

What comes out is clearer. It is not safe. Boil it.

## Chemical treatment

Tablets and drops are light, cheap and slow. They need contact time — read the packet, and double it in cold water. They work poorly in cloudy water, which is another argument for filtering the sediment out first.

## The order that works

1. Choose the best source you can reach.
2. Let it settle, then pour off the clear part.
3. Filter out the sediment through cloth.
4. Boil, or treat.

## Which method for which situation

**You have a stove and fuel:** boil. It handles every biological threat, needs no spare parts, and there is nothing to break.

**You are moving and want water now:** a filter. Fast, no waiting, no taste. Check what it is rated to remove and keep it from freezing — ice destroys the internal fibres, and a frozen filter can fail silently afterwards.

**You want the lightest possible backup:** tablets. Almost no weight, no moving parts, and they work while you walk. The cost is waiting time and a taste most people notice.

**Best practice on a long trip:** carry two methods that fail differently. A filter plus tablets covers a clogged cartridge; a stove plus tablets covers running out of fuel.

## What none of these fix

Treatment deals with living organisms. It does not deal with chemical contamination, agricultural runoff, or heavy metals — boiling actually concentrates those slightly as water evaporates.

That is why source choice comes before treatment. Moving water above human activity beats standing water below a farm, a mine or a road, and no amount of boiling changes that ranking.

## Water you should not drink at all

- **Water near mining, industry or intensive agriculture**, however clear.
- **Salt water.** Drinking it costs you more water than it provides.
- **Urine.** It concentrates the waste your body just removed.
- **Water from a stagnant pool with no inlet or outlet**, if anything else is reachable.

## Rain, snow and dew

Rain caught directly in a clean container is about the safest water available outdoors, and a tarp funnelled into a bottle collects a surprising amount quickly.

Snow and ice must be melted first — eating snow costs you body heat exactly when you cannot spare it, and it is a real contributor to getting cold. Melt it with a little liquid water already in the pot so it does not scorch.

Dew wiped off clean vegetation with a cloth at dawn is slow but real, and in dry country it can be the difference.

## Finding water in the first place

Follow the landscape downhill. Look for greener, denser vegetation than the ground around it. Check the outside bend of a dry watercourse and dig at the lowest shaded point. Insect traffic and converging animal tracks are better indicators than any single plant.

Dig in shade, in the cool part of the day. Sweating out more water than you find is a real risk in dry country.
