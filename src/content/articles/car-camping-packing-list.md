Car camping means you're not carrying gear on your back, so the packing question changes from "what's the lightest option" to "what actually improves the trip" — and the most common mistake is either treating it like backpacking and leaving comfort items behind, or overloading the car with things that never get used.

## Clothing (more room to layer properly)

Since weight isn't a constraint, bring a full base/mid/shell layering set plus a genuine change of clothes for evening — sitting around a cool campsite after dark is very different from hiking through it, and dry, warmer clothes for camp make the evening far more comfortable.

This is the single biggest difference between car camping and any other kind of trip. Backpackers move most of the day and generate their own heat. Car campers sit still for hours after dark, doing almost nothing, which is when the temperature drops fastest. Pack for sitting outside in the cold, not for walking in it — that means a warmer layer than you think, plus a hat, and something on your feet other than the boots you walked in.

## Comfort items worth the space

- A real camp chair (not optional after the first uncomfortable trip)
- A cooler with ice for actual perishable food, not just snacks
- A <a href="https://s.click.aliexpress.com/e/_c3vG78et" rel="sponsored noopener" target="_blank">compact folding table</a> for cooking prep and eating, useful when the site doesn't guarantee a picnic table
- A larger tent with standing room, if your vehicle allows it
- Extra blankets — cheaper and warmer than another layer of technical clothing for sitting around camp
- A proper foam or thick inflatable sleeping mat, since bulk costs you nothing here
- A rug or offcut of carpet for the tent doorway, which keeps a startling amount of dirt out
- A head torch per person, plus one lantern for the group

## The things people forget every single time

Almost every miserable car-camping story traces back to one of these rather than to a missing tent:

- **Enough light.** One torch shared between four people means three people sitting in the dark.
- **A way to deal with rain.** A tarp or shelter over the cooking area turns a washed-out weekend into a normal one.
- **Rubbish bags**, and a plan for what to do with waste and food scraps.
- **A first-aid kit with blister and burn cover.** Camp injuries are overwhelmingly small cuts, burns from the stove and fire, and blisters.
- **Something to sit on that is not the ground**, which is the chair point again because it matters that much.
- **A spare tent peg and a length of cord.** Both weigh nothing and both save a night.

## Cooking setup

Car camping supports a real cooking setup: a two-burner stove, a full cookware set, and fresh ingredients that wouldn't survive a backpacking trip. This is where a proper foil-packet dinner or a full skillet meal makes sense, versus the minimal one-pot meals a backpacker is limited to.

Two things make camp cooking dramatically easier, and neither is equipment. First, prepare at home: chop the vegetables, mix the marinade, portion the dry ingredients into bags. Cooking at a picnic table with limited light and no running water is not the place to be dicing onions. Second, plan washing-up before you plan the menu. A meal that dirties three pans is a meal that ends with you scrubbing in cold water in the dark.

## Water, and where it comes from

Check before you go whether the site has drinking water. If it does, bring containers to carry it from the tap to your pitch. If it does not, you are carrying every litre for drinking, cooking and washing, and that adds up fast for a group over a weekend.

A large container with a tap, kept at the pitch, is one of the highest-value car-camping items precisely because it is too bulky to consider on any other kind of trip.

## Load the car so you can find things

Pack in the reverse order you will need things. The tent, mallet and a torch should come out first, not be buried under the cooler. Group items into boxes by function — kitchen, sleep, tools — rather than filling gaps at random, because arriving in the dark and rain and having to unload the whole car to find the pegs is a genuinely bad start.

Keep one small bag accessible with the things you want during the drive and immediately on arrival: torch, snacks, waterproofs, keys, first aid.

## What still becomes clutter

More space doesn't mean unlimited gear. Duplicate tools, a second tent "in case," and enough clothing for twice the trip length are the car-camping version of overpacking — you'll spend more time organizing the car than you saved by not thinking it through.

There is a real cost to this beyond tidiness. Every extra box is something to load, unload, keep dry, search through and repack. Groups that bring less consistently spend more of the weekend sitting by the fire and less of it moving boxes around.

## A simple filter

If an item makes the specific evenings and mornings of this trip better, bring it. If it's just "nice to have" with no clear use on this trip, it's using space that could hold something you'll actually use.

And one last check before you drive off: does anyone outside the group know where you are going and when you are back? Car camping feels safe enough that people skip this, but a campsite in a valley with no phone signal is further from help than it looks on the map.

## Quick answers

**What should I bring for car camping that I would not bring backpacking?**
Comfort items weight would normally rule out — a real camp chair, a cooler, extra clothing layers, and a larger stove — since the car carries the weight instead of you.

**What do people forget when car camping?**
Telling someone where they are going and when they are back. Car camping feels close to safety, but a valley site with no signal is often further from help than it looks.

**How much food should I bring car camping?**
More flexibility than backpacking allows, but still worth planning meal by meal rather than guessing — a cooler makes over-packing food easy and wasteful.

**Is car camping good for beginners?**
Yes — it removes the weight constraint of backpacking, which makes it a lower-pressure way to learn what you actually use before carrying it on your back.
