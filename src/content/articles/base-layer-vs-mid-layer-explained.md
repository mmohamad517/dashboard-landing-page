A base layer's job is moving sweat off your skin so you stay dry; a mid layer's job is trapping the warm air your body produces so you stay warm. They solve different problems, which is why swapping a thick base layer in for a mid layer (or the reverse) usually leaves you either damp or cold, even though both are "just a shirt" on the surface.

## Base layer: the moisture manager

Worn directly against your skin, on every hike regardless of temperature. A good base layer is close-fitting (so it can actually contact your skin and wick moisture) and made from merino wool or a synthetic wicking fabric — never cotton, which absorbs and holds moisture instead of moving it away. Weight varies by season: a lightweight base layer in summer, a heavier one in winter, but the job stays the same in both cases.

The mechanism is worth understanding, because it explains every piece of advice that follows. Sweat cools you by evaporating. Evaporation happens at a surface exposed to air. A wicking fabric spreads liquid sweat across a wide area of fabric and moves it outward, away from skin, where it can evaporate from the outside of the garment instead of sitting on you. Cotton does the opposite: it absorbs the moisture into the fibre and holds it right where it will keep pulling heat out of you long after you have stopped working.

## Mid layer: the heat trapper

Worn over the base layer, added and removed based on activity level rather than kept on all day. Fleece, wool sweaters, and light synthetic or down puffy jackets all work as mid layers because they trap air in their loft — that trapped air, not the fabric itself, is what insulates you. This is why a compressed, packed-away puffy jacket doesn't keep you warm: it needs to loft up to trap air.

Because the insulation is the air, anything that squashes the loft reduces the warmth. A mid layer worn under a tight shell, or under pack straps, insulates less at those points. Two thin mid layers usually beat one thick one for the same reason a double-glazed window beats a single thick pane: you get more separate air spaces, and you can remove half of it when the climb starts.

## Fit: snug versus roomy

This is the practical difference you can see in a shop. A base layer should be close to the skin — not compressive, but with no loose bagging, because fabric that isn't touching you isn't wicking from you. A mid layer wants the opposite: enough room to loft, and enough room to go on over the base layer without flattening it.

The knock-on effect is that layers have to be bought as a system, or at least tried on as one. A shell that fits beautifully over a t-shirt and won't close over your fleece is a shell you will never wear on the days you need it.

## Why the order and timing matter

A mid layer worn directly against skin does a poor job of managing sweat, and a base layer worn as your only layer in cold conditions doesn't trap enough heat. Worse, wearing your mid layer non-stop through a climb means you sweat into it, and wet insulation loses most of its warming ability — so the "warm layer" ends up making you colder later, once you stop moving and the dampness catches up with you.

This is the single most common sequence that turns a good day bad. You start cold, keep the fleece on to feel comfortable, work hard for an hour, arrive at the top damp through both layers, stop moving, and the wind finds you. Nothing in your pack fixes that quickly. Taking the fleece off at the bottom of the climb would have.

## Down and synthetic mid layers behave differently when wet

If your mid layer is an insulated jacket rather than a fleece, the fill material changes how much a mistake costs you. Down gives excellent warmth for its weight and packs very small, but it loses loft when it gets wet and takes a long time to recover. Synthetic insulation is bulkier and heavier for the same warmth, and it keeps insulating better when damp and dries faster.

Neither is simply better. Down suits cold, dry conditions and trips where pack space is tight. Synthetic suits wet climates and anyone who expects their layer to get damp. Fleece sits somewhere in between: heavier and bulkier than both, cheap, tough, and almost indifferent to moisture.

## A simple way to remember the difference

Base layer = manages moisture, worn always. Mid layer = manages warmth, worn situationally. If you're taking a layer on and off throughout the hike, it's doing a mid-layer job. If it's on your skin the entire time, it's doing a base-layer job. Treating either one as if it does both jobs is the root of most cold, damp, or overheated hikes.

## The "thick base layer" trap

A heavyweight base layer looks like it should do both jobs at once, and this is where people get caught. It does wick, and it does add some warmth, so on a cold day it feels like a sensible single garment. The problem is that you cannot take it off. When the climb starts and you overheat, your only options are to open a zip or to strip to nothing — so you sweat into the one layer that is supposed to stay dry, and you have no way to reduce insulation without removing your moisture management entirely.

A light base layer plus a separate fleece gives the same warmth standing still, and one extra option while moving. The extra option is the whole point.

## Where cotton is genuinely fine

To be fair to cotton: it is comfortable, cheap, and perfectly reasonable for a warm, dry, low-effort walk where you are never far from the car, and for sleeping in at a campsite where you are not producing sweat. The advice to avoid it is about conditions where getting wet has a real cost — cold, wind, exposure, or distance from help. Knowing why the rule exists tells you when it does not apply.

## Washing: the part that quietly ruins both

Base layers lose wicking performance as body oils and detergent residue clog the fibres, so they need washing more often than they smell like they do. Fabric softener is worse than useless on technical fabrics — it coats exactly the fibres that move moisture. Follow the garment label, skip the softener, and both layers will behave like new kit for years.

For mid layers, the thing that kills them is heat. High dryer settings can damage fleece pile and melt synthetic insulation, and once the loft is gone the warmth is gone with it.

## A one-line test for anything in your wardrobe

Hold the garment up and ask which job it is doing. Is it against skin, moving moisture? Base layer. Is it holding air, and will you take it off when you start working? Mid layer. Is it keeping wind and water out? Shell. Anything that cannot answer clearly is probably a compromise, and compromises are exactly what leave you damp at the top of the climb.
