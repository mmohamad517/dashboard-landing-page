A base layer's job is moving sweat off your skin so you stay dry; a mid layer's job is trapping the warm air your body produces so you stay warm. They solve different problems, which is why swapping a thick base layer in for a mid layer (or the reverse) usually leaves you either damp or cold, even though both are "just a shirt" on the surface.

## Base layer: the moisture manager

Worn directly against your skin, on every hike regardless of temperature. A good base layer is close-fitting (so it can actually contact your skin and wick moisture) and made from merino wool or a synthetic wicking fabric — never cotton, which absorbs and holds moisture instead of moving it away. Weight varies by season: a lightweight base layer in summer, a heavier one in winter, but the job stays the same in both cases.

## Mid layer: the heat trapper

Worn over the base layer, added and removed based on activity level rather than kept on all day. Fleece, wool sweaters, and light synthetic or down puffy jackets all work as mid layers because they trap air in their loft — that trapped air, not the fabric itself, is what insulates you. This is why a compressed, packed-away puffy jacket doesn't keep you warm: it needs to loft up to trap air.

## Why the order and timing matter

A mid layer worn directly against skin does a poor job of managing sweat, and a base layer worn as your only layer in cold conditions doesn't trap enough heat. Worse, wearing your mid layer non-stop through a climb means you sweat into it, and wet insulation loses most of its warming ability — so the "warm layer" ends up making you colder later, once you stop moving and the dampness catches up with you.

## A simple way to remember the difference

Base layer = manages moisture, worn always. Mid layer = manages warmth, worn situationally. If you're taking a layer on and off throughout the hike, it's doing a mid-layer job. If it's on your skin the entire time, it's doing a base-layer job. Treating either one as if it does both jobs is the root of most cold, damp, or overheated hikes.
