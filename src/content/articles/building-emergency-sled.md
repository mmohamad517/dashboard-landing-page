Start with the order of operations, because it matters more than the sled itself: stabilize the person, keep them warm, and [signal for help](/articles/how-to-signal-for-help-in-the-wilderness/) before you consider moving them. An emergency sled is what you build when someone can't walk out, the ground is snow or packed enough to drag a load over, and you genuinely cannot wait for rescue to reach you — not a default plan for getting someone back to the car faster.

## When not to move someone at all

This is the part to get right before anything else. If there's any possibility of a neck or spinal injury — a fall, a blow to the head or back, or the person can't say clearly whether they can feel and move all four limbs — moving them wrongly can turn a survivable injury into a permanent one. The [first aid basics](/articles/camping-first-aid-basics/) guide covers the signs that mean stop and call rather than continue treating something yourself, and that rule applies here first.

Unless staying in place is the more dangerous option — genuine exposure risk, an unstable slope above you, or a real threat you can't wait out — the safer choice is almost always to keep the person still, keep them warm, and get help to come to you rather than moving them yourself. This article covers what to do when moving them really is the only option left.

## What you actually need

- **Something rigid or semi-rigid and broad enough to spread weight**, so the person doesn't sink into or drag against the snow — a tent floor, a stiff tarp, a sleeping pad, or skis or a snowboard lashed together side by side all work.
- **Cordage or straps** to secure the person to it and to build a pull point.
- **Insulation between the person and the sled surface.** The same principle that applies to sleeping on cold ground applies here — [insulating underneath matters as much as covering someone on top](/articles/cold-weather-survival-basics/), and a person lying directly on a tarp over snow loses heat fast.

None of this needs to be purpose-built gear. What you have with you, used deliberately, is the actual kit.

## Basic build principles

- **A rigid base first.** Whatever you're using needs to hold its shape under weight rather than folding at the middle, or the person ends up dragging through snow rather than sliding over it.
- **Insulate, then secure.** Pad and insulate the surface before you lift anyone onto it, then secure them well enough that they can't slide or roll off on uneven ground — without anything tight enough to restrict breathing or circulation.
- **Build a pull point that tracks straight.** A harness or line attached at a single centered point, rather than to a corner, keeps the sled following the puller instead of fishtailing sideways on a slope.
- **Check warmth and circulation before moving**, and again at every stop. A person who is strapped down and can't move their own limbs will get cold faster than one who was walking, so watch for this actively rather than assuming the insulation is enough on its own.

## Moving it safely

Go slower than feels necessary, and test terrain before committing the person to it — walk a steep or uncertain section first if you can, rather than finding out its condition with them already on the sled. More than one person makes this dramatically safer: one pulling, one behind steadying and controlling speed on any descent, since an unguided sled can outrun the person pulling it on a slope.

Stop regularly to recheck the person's condition, their warmth, and that nothing securing them has shifted or tightened. A short trip taken carefully beats a fast one that arrives with a second injury.

## When to stop and call instead

If the terrain ahead is steeper or more exposed than what you've already crossed, if the person's condition changes for the worse, or if there's any way to reach help and wait for it, that is almost always the better choice over continuing an improvised evacuation. This technique exists for the situation where none of those options are available — not as a substitute for calling for a rescue that can actually reach you.

None of this replaces a wilderness first aid course, which is where this is taught properly, with hands-on practice and someone correcting your technique in real time.

## Quick answers

**When should you build an emergency sled instead of waiting for help?**
Only when the person can't walk, the ground allows dragging a load, and you genuinely cannot wait for rescue to reach you — waiting and signaling for help is the safer choice whenever it's actually available.

**What can I use to build an emergency sled?**
Something rigid enough to spread weight and not sink into snow — a tent floor, a stiff tarp, a sleeping pad, or skis or a snowboard lashed side by side — plus cordage or straps to secure the person and build a pull point.

**Is it safe to move someone with a possible spinal injury on a sled?**
Not unless staying in place is genuinely more dangerous than moving them. Any possibility of a neck or back injury means stop and call for help rather than moving them yourself — see the [first aid basics](/articles/camping-first-aid-basics/) guide for the signs that mean this.

**How many people does it take to safely pull an emergency sled?**
More than one if at all possible — one to pull and one to steady and control speed from behind, especially on any downhill section, since an unguided sled can outrun the person pulling it.
