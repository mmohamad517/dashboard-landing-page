A day hike packing list needs to cover four things: layering for the weather, enough water and food for the distance, basic safety items, and nothing else — the biggest overpacking mistake on short hikes is bringing overnight-trip gear for a three-hour walk.

## Clothing

- Moisture-wicking base layer top and bottoms (no cotton)
- Packable mid layer (fleece or light insulated vest) — carried, not worn, at the trailhead
- Packable wind/rain shell
- Sun protection: hat and buff, even under cloud cover above the treeline
- One extra pair of socks in a dry bag — the single most commonly forgotten item, and a leading cause of blisters

Note the word *carried* on the mid layer. If you are comfortable standing at the trailhead you are dressed for standing still, not for walking uphill, and you will be sweating within a few minutes of starting. Start slightly cold and put the layer on when you stop.

## Water and food

Two liters of water is a reasonable starting point for a moderate day hike, and it is a starting point rather than a rule — heat, elevation, how hard you are working and how much you personally sweat all move the real number, in some cases a lot. Look at the route for refill points before you go, and if there are none, carry the whole day's supply.

For food, simple and calorie-dense beats elaborate: trail mix, an energy bar for the halfway point, a sandwich, and an electrolyte packet if you're sweating heavily (cramps are often a salt problem, not just a water problem).

Eat before you feel hungry and drink before you feel thirsty. Both sensations lag behind the actual need, and on a hot climb the gap is wide enough to matter. Dehydration takes your judgement well before it takes your legs, which is why so many bad decisions get made in the last hour of a long day.

## Safety basics — the ones that earn their weight

- **A map or downloaded offline trail map** — don't rely on cell signal. Screenshot the route as well; a screenshot survives a dead data connection, a browser tab does not.
- **A basic first-aid kit.** Blister care matters more than most other items in it, and blister care used early beats blister care used late.
- **A headlamp**, even for a hike you plan to finish before dark. Being caught out at dusk is common; being caught out at dusk without light is what turns it into a problem.
- **A fully charged phone**, and a battery pack if the day is long. Cold weather drains batteries faster, so keep the phone somewhere warm.
- **A whistle.** Almost weightless, carries much further than your voice, and works when you are exhausted.

## What to leave home

Full cook systems, tent stakes, a sleeping bag, and a second complete outfit belong on an overnight trip, not a day hike. If you're carrying gear you can't name a specific use for on this exact trip, it's dead weight.

The same applies to the enormous "ten essentials"-style lists that circulate online. Most of them are written to cover every hike anyone might ever do, from a suburban loop to a remote alpine traverse. Carrying all of it on a two-hour woodland walk is not caution, it is just a heavy bag — and a heavy bag makes you slower, which is its own risk.

## Tell someone where you are going

This is the item that weighs nothing and matters most, and it is not gear. One person should know your route and roughly when you expect to be back. A search that starts with a known route and a known return time is short. One that starts with "he mentioned something about the hills" is not.

Send it as a message so it is written down rather than remembered, and tell them when you are safely out.

## Adjust the list for the actual day

The list above is a baseline, not a fixed kit. Three questions change it:

**How long, and how remote?** A busy trail an hour from a road forgives mistakes that a quiet route four hours out does not. Distance from help is the real variable, not distance walked.

**What is the forecast doing later?** Not at the trailhead now — later, and higher up. Conditions on exposed ground are colder and much windier than at the car park.

**Who is with you?** Children, someone new to walking, or a dog all slow the group and widen the range of what can go wrong. Pack for the slowest person, not for yourself.

## Pack it so you will actually use it

Anything you need while walking — shell, water, snacks, map, sunscreen — goes in a lid pocket, hip-belt pocket or side pocket. Anything buried at the bottom of the bag is effectively not with you, because in wind and drizzle almost nobody stops, takes the pack off and digs. They push on and get cold instead.

Put the spare socks and anything that must stay dry in a sealed bag, even inside a "waterproof" pack. Pack covers keep rain off the outside and do nothing about the rain running down your back into the top.

## A simple way to decide

Ask what happens if the weather turns, you're out longer than planned, or someone gets a minor injury — then pack for those three scenarios and stop. That's the whole list.

Those three cover nearly everything that actually goes wrong on a day hike. Anything that does not serve one of them is a candidate to leave at home, and anything that serves two is worth its weight twice over — which is why a shell, a headlamp and a bag of food end up on every version of this list ever written.
