Almost every failed fire is a tinder failure, not a spark failure. People strike sparks into material that was never going to catch, run out of patience, and conclude the technique does not work.

## Build the whole fire before you make a spark

1. A nest of the finest dry material you can find — dead grass, birch bark shavings, cattail down, tinder fungus, or cotton wool from your kit.
2. Pencil-lead thin kindling, a good handful.
3. Pencil-thick sticks.
4. Thumb-thick sticks.

All of it stacked and within reach *before* you strike anything. When the nest catches you will have seconds, not minutes.

## Ferro rod

The most reliable method that does not need fuel. It works soaked, works cold, costs very little, and lasts for thousands of strikes.

Hold the rod still and pull the striker back along it, rather than jabbing at the rod — that way you do not scatter your tinder nest. Shower the sparks into the nest, then lift the nest and breathe into it gently until it flames.

## Magnifying lens

Works well in direct sun and not at all otherwise. Focus the smallest, brightest point you can onto dark, dry tinder and hold it steady. A camera lens, a clear bottle of water, or spectacles will all do it in good light.

## Friction fire, honestly

A bow drill is a real skill and worth learning. It is also the wrong thing to depend on. Most people need many attempts in ideal conditions with correctly chosen wood before they get an ember, and wet weather makes it dramatically harder.

Learn it at home, for the satisfaction and because the understanding transfers. Carry a ferro rod for the trip.

## Fire rules come first

Fire regulations vary enormously by region and season, and in many places open fires are banned outright for part of the year. Check before you light anything, keep water within reach, and never leave a flame unattended.
