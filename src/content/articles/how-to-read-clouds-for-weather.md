Sky reading buys you minutes to hours of warning. It does not replace a forecast, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. Check a real forecast before you leave, then watch the sky for what it changes.

## The sequence that matters

Most weather deterioration follows a recognisable order:

1. **High, wispy cloud** appears, often ahead of a front.
2. **Cloud thickens and lowers** — the front is arriving.
3. **Cloud becomes uniform and grey**, and light rain starts.

Each step is a chance to make a decision while you still have options.

## The one to take seriously

Towering cloud with a flat, spreading top — the classic anvil — means a thunderstorm. This is not a "watch it for a while" situation. Get off ridges, summits and exposed ground, away from lone tall trees, and out of water.

**If you can hear thunder at all, you are close enough to be struck.**

## Wind

A wind that swings direction and then drops often means a front is passing. Rising wind with lowering cloud usually means it is getting worse before better. On ridges and in valleys, local terrain does strange things to wind, so treat this as a hint rather than a rule.

## Fair weather signs

Small, scattered, puffy clouds with clear gaps that stay small through the day usually mean a settled few hours. The watch item is whether they start growing vertically through the afternoon — that is how storms build in mountain country.

## What sky reading cannot do

It cannot tell you what is happening on the other side of a ridge. It cannot see rain falling in hills upstream of you, which is exactly what causes [flash floods](/articles/flash-flood-safety-for-campers/). And it gives you no useful warning at night.

## The habit that matters more

Check the forecast for your actual destination and elevation, not the nearest town. Mountain weather at 1,500 metres is not valley weather. Set a turnaround time based on it, and honour that time.
