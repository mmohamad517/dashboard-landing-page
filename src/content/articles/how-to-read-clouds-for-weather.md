Sky reading buys you minutes to hours of warning. It does not replace a forecast, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. Check a real forecast before you leave, then watch the sky for what it changes.

## The sequence that matters

Most weather deterioration follows a recognisable order:

1. **High, wispy cloud** appears, often ahead of a front.
2. **Cloud thickens and lowers** — the front is arriving.
3. **Cloud becomes uniform and grey**, and light rain starts.

Each step is a chance to make a decision while you still have options.

## The one to take seriously

Towering cloud with a flat, spreading top — the classic anvil — means a thunderstorm. This is not a "watch it for a while" situation. Get off ridges, summits and exposed ground, away from lone tall trees, and out of water.

**If you can hear thunder at all, you are close enough to be struck.**

## Wind

A wind that swings direction and then drops often means a front is passing. Rising wind with lowering cloud usually means it is getting worse before better. On ridges and in valleys, local terrain does strange things to wind, so treat this as a hint rather than a rule.

## Fair weather signs

Small, scattered, puffy clouds with clear gaps that stay small through the day usually mean a settled few hours. The watch item is whether they start growing vertically through the afternoon — that is how storms build in mountain country.

## What sky reading cannot do

It cannot tell you what is happening on the other side of a ridge. It cannot see rain falling in hills upstream of you, which is exactly what causes [flash floods](/articles/flash-flood-safety-for-campers/). And it gives you no useful warning at night.

## What to do about a thunderstorm

Since this is the one cloud sign that can genuinely hurt you, it is worth being concrete.

Leave high, exposed ground early rather than late. Get off summits and ridges, away from lone tall trees, away from water and away from metal — fences, poles, via ferrata cable. Lower ground among trees of even height is better than the tallest thing in an open space.

Spread the group out rather than sheltering together, so one strike cannot affect everyone. If you are caught in the open with nowhere better, crouch low on the balls of your feet with your feet together, making yourself small rather than lying flat.

Wait a good while after the last thunder before moving back onto exposed ground. Storms come in waves, and the gap between cells feels much safer than it is.

## Mountains make their own weather

Air forced up over high ground cools and forms cloud, which is why a summit can be in cloud and rain while the valley below stays sunny. It is also why afternoon storms are so common in mountain country — the ground heats through the morning, air rises, and cloud that was small at ten o'clock is towering by two.

The practical consequence is a habit rather than an observation: in mountains, start early and be off the high ground by early afternoon. That single rule avoids more bad weather than any amount of sky reading.

## Lenticular and cap clouds

A smooth, lens-shaped cloud sitting motionless over or downwind of a peak, or a cap sitting on the summit itself, indicates strong wind aloft even when it feels calm where you are standing.

It looks calm because the cloud is not moving — but it is stationary while air rushes through it. If you were planning to go higher, treat it as a reason to check the wind forecast for the top rather than the bottom.

## Temperature, damp and visibility

Two other signs worth noticing. A sudden drop in temperature with rising wind often means a front arriving. And when distant hills look unusually sharp and close, the air is very clear and often very moist — in many places that precedes rain rather than confirming good weather.

Neither is reliable enough to act on alone. Both are worth adding to the picture.

## The habit that matters more

Check the forecast for your actual destination and elevation, not the nearest town. Mountain weather at 1,500 metres is not valley weather. Set a turnaround time based on it, and honour that time.
