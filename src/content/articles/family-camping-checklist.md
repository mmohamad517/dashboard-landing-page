Camping with kids is not "the same list, but more of it." A few items matter far more than they do for adults, a few new ones appear that never make an adult-only list, and a few adult essentials quietly become optional because the trip is built around the kids' pace, not yours. Packing the standard list and just doubling the quantities is how people end up over-loaded and still missing the one thing that actually mattered.

## What changes with kids on the trip

Three things shift the whole list: kids lose heat and get sunburned faster than adults notice in themselves, a bored child at 4pm becomes everyone's problem, and a minor scrape that an adult would shrug off can derail a child's whole trip if there's no easy fix on hand. Everything below follows from those three facts, not from packing more of the adult list.

## Sleep setup for a family tent

- A sleeping bag rated for the child, not a hand-me-down adult bag they're swimming in — a bag that's too roomy has more empty air to warm, which makes a child colder, not more comfortable
- A sleeping pad under each child, same as the adults — kids sleep on the ground too, and cold ground pulls heat out of a small body faster than a large one
- A small, dim light kept within reach — a headlamp on its lowest setting or a battery lantern — so a child who wakes at 2am isn't fumbling in total dark in an unfamiliar tent
- Whatever comfort item goes everywhere at home goes in the tent bag first, not packed last

A tent that felt spacious for two adults can feel crowded and disorienting to a small child at night. Walking them through where they are sleeping and where the exit is, while it's still light out, removes most of the 2am confusion before it starts.

## Clothing: more spares, not more layers

The layering system itself doesn't change — base, mid, and a shell still cover a child the same way it covers an adult. What changes is the spares. Kids get wet, muddy, or sandy faster than adults and have less patience for staying in damp clothes, so:

- One full spare outfit per day, not per trip
- A sun hat and sunscreen, reapplied more often than feels necessary — kids often don't report sunburn until it's already happened
- Warm layers for the evening are non-negotiable, even in a season that feels mild during the day — a child who's been running around all day cools down fast the moment they stop

## The health and safety extras

The first-aid kit for a family trip needs a few additions beyond the basics: children's-strength pain and fever medication if your family already uses it at home, a larger supply of adhesive bandages than seems reasonable (they get used on scrapes that wouldn't warrant one at home), and insect repellent suitable for the ages of the kids on the trip.

A whistle for each child old enough to understand what it's for is worth more than it looks. A whistle carries much further than a shouting voice, and a distressed child can blow one long after they've stopped being able to shout. Pair it with one simple rule they can actually follow: if you can't see an adult, stop moving and blow the whistle — don't go looking.

## Food that doesn't fight the trip

Familiar food travels better than ambitious food on a family trip. This isn't the weekend to introduce a child to camp cooking they've never had at home — pack meals and snacks they already eat and like, plus more snacks than seems necessary, since kids run through energy in bursts and get disproportionately difficult when hungry. More water than you'd pack for adults alone, and a way to remind kids to drink it, since thirst signals are easy for a busy child to ignore until it's a problem.

## What parents forget most

- **Baby wipes** — for hands, faces, and the general mess of outdoor living with a child, whether or not there's a baby on the trip
- **Extra plastic bags**, more than you think — wet clothes, dirty clothes, and rubbish multiply fast with kids around
- **A written note of any allergies or medication**, kept with the first-aid kit, in case another adult on the trip needs it and you're not immediately reachable
- **Something familiar from home** — even one object lowers the strangeness of an unfamiliar place for a young child at bedtime

## A realistic family camping checklist

Everything above, condensed:

- Rated sleeping bag and pad per child, plus a low-light option for the night
- One full spare outfit per child per day, sun hat, sunscreen, and a warm evening layer
- First-aid kit with children's medication, extra bandages, and age-appropriate insect repellent
- A whistle per child old enough to use one, and the one rule that goes with it
- Familiar food, more snacks than feels necessary, and reminders to drink water
- Baby wipes, extra bags, a written note of allergies or medication, one item from home

## Quick answers

**What's different about packing for camping with kids versus adults?**
Not more of the same list — different priorities. Kids lose heat and sunburn faster, get bored faster, and need small health extras (children's medication, more bandages) that never make an adult-only packing list.

**What's the most commonly forgotten item on a family camping trip?**
Baby wipes and a genuinely dim light source for the tent at night — both feel minor until the first night without them.

**Do kids need their own first-aid supplies?**
Add children's-strength medication if your family uses it, extra adhesive bandages, and age-appropriate insect repellent to the standard kit rather than assuming the adult kit covers it.
