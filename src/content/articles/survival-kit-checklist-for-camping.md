A survival kit list is only useful if you can say what each item prevents. Most lists are organised by object type — fire, tools, shelter — which tells you what to buy but not why. This one is organised by what actually goes wrong, so you can see instantly whether your kit covers it or not.

## Start with the failure, not the object

Almost every bad day outdoors comes down to a small number of problems: you got cold, you got wet, you got lost, nobody could find you, somebody got hurt, or you ran out of water. Kit is only worth carrying if it answers one of those.

That framing does two things. It stops you buying novelty items that answer no real question, and it shows you the gaps — most people carry three ways to make fire and no way to be seen.

## Being cold

- **Foil blanket or bivvy bag.** Weighs almost nothing. Turns a bad night into an uncomfortable one.
- **Spare warm layer**, kept dry in a bag.
- **Hat and gloves**, even in summer at elevation.
- **Dry socks**, sealed.

The bivvy bag is the most under-rated item on this page. A foil blanket is better than nothing but tears and flaps in wind; a bivvy bag is a windproof, waterproof sack you climb inside, costs little, and packs to the size of a fist. If you are stationary and cold — waiting with an injured person, or benighted — it is the single item that changes the outcome most.

## Being wet

- **Waterproof shell** you can reach without unpacking.
- **Dry bags** or bin liners for the layers you are keeping dry.
- **Pack cover**, or a liner inside the pack.

A liner inside the pack beats a cover over it. Covers deal with rain landing on top and do nothing about the water running down your back and in through the opening, which is how "waterproof" packs end up with a wet sleeping bag inside.

## Being lost

- **Offline map downloaded** and route screenshotted.
- **Compass**, and enough practice to use it.
- **Power bank**, charged.
- **Head torch** with spare batteries — not your phone light.

Note the phrasing on the compass. A compass you have never used is decoration; it takes one afternoon in a park to learn enough to be useful. And the screenshot matters because it survives a dead data connection in a way a browser tab does not.

The head torch is not interchangeable with a phone light. Your phone is your map, your communication and your emergency call — draining it for light is trading the important thing for the cheap one.

## Being found

- **Whistle** on your pack strap.
- **Small mirror** or foil panel.
- **Bright item** that can be spread out.

This is the category people skip, and it is the cheapest one on the page. A whistle costs almost nothing, weighs almost nothing, carries much further than a shout, and works when you are exhausted or hoarse. Put it on the strap, not in the pack — a whistle you have to dig for is a whistle you will not use.

## Injury

- **First aid kit** with plenty of dressings, tape, blister care and gloves.
- **Any personal medication** the group needs.
- **Trekking pole** — doubles as a crutch or shelter pole.

Buy or build a kit heavy on the boring things. Real outdoor injuries are overwhelmingly blisters, small cuts, burns from a stove or fire, and sprains. Nobody has ever wished for fewer dressings or less tape.

And the kit is not the skill. A first aid kit in the hands of someone who has never done a course is a bag of supplies. If you take one action after reading this page, book a course.

## Water and food

- **More water than you think**, plus a way to treat more.
- **A day's spare food** that needs no cooking.

Water is the one you cannot wait out. You can go a long time without food and a short time dehydrated, and dehydration takes your judgement before it takes your legs. Know where you would refill before you go, and carry a filter or tablets rather than the whole day's weight where sources exist.

The spare food should need no cooking, because the scenario where you need it is the scenario where you have no stove, no fuel, or no time.

## Fire

- **Ferro rod** and a lighter. Two methods, both light.
- **Prepared tinder** in a waterproof bag.

Two methods rather than one, because a lighter fails when wet or cold and a ferro rod works soaked. The prepared tinder matters more than either: almost every failed fire is a tinder failure, not a spark failure, and finding dry tinder in the rain when you are already cold is the hardest part of the job.

## Repairs

- **Duct tape** wrapped round a pole rather than a full roll.
- **Cord** — a few metres.
- **Safety pins**, a needle and thread.
- **Knife or multi-tool.**

A broken pack strap, a snapped tent pole or a split boot sole ends a trip as effectively as weather does, and all three are fixable with tape and cord.

## What this list deliberately leaves out

**Anything for catching or identifying food.** Fishing kits in a tin and foraging guides answer a problem you will not have. A healthy adult can go a long time without eating; the effort belongs on staying warm and being found. And no printed guide makes wild plant or mushroom identification safe.

**Weapons.** They answer a scenario that is vanishingly rare compared to hypothermia and getting lost, and carrying one changes nothing about the ways trips actually go wrong.

**Anything you have never used.** Kit you have not tried is closer to decoration than equipment.

## An honest note

Some popular kit is mostly reassurance. Survival playing cards with instructions printed too small to read, novelty wire saws, and fishing kits in a tin will not change the outcome of a bad day. The things that will: [telling someone your route](/articles/common-camping-mistakes-beginners-make/), a charged phone, a dry layer, and a whistle.

Weight matters. A pack so heavy it ends the trip early has its own risk.

## Build it once, then check it

Assemble the kit, then leave it assembled in its own bag so it moves between trips without being unpacked. Twice a year, open it and check three things: batteries, the expiry dates in the first aid kit, and whether the lighter still lights. All three fail silently, and all three fail at the worst moment.

Then, once, use it. Light the ferro rod. Blow the whistle. Get inside the bivvy bag in the garden. Ten minutes now is the difference between carrying equipment and carrying objects.
