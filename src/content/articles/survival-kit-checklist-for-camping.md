A kit list is only useful if you can say what each item prevents. Here is the list organised that way.

## Being cold

- **Foil blanket or bivvy bag.** Weighs almost nothing. Turns a bad night into an uncomfortable one.
- **Spare warm layer**, kept dry in a bag.
- **Hat and gloves**, even in summer at elevation.
- **Dry socks**, sealed.

## Being wet

- **Waterproof shell** you can reach without unpacking.
- **Dry bags** or bin liners for the layers you are keeping dry.
- **Pack cover**, or a liner inside the pack.

## Being lost

- **Offline map downloaded** and route screenshotted.
- **Compass**, and enough practice to use it.
- **Power bank**, charged.
- **Head torch** with spare batteries — not your phone light.

## Being found

- **Whistle** on your pack strap.
- **Small mirror** or foil panel.
- **Bright item** that can be spread out.

## Injury

- **First aid kit** with plenty of dressings, tape, blister care and gloves.
- **Any personal medication** the group needs.
- **Trekking pole** — doubles as a crutch or shelter pole.

## Water and food

- **More water than you think**, plus a way to treat more.
- **A day's spare food** that needs no cooking.

## Fire

- **Ferro rod** and a lighter. Two methods, both light.
- **Prepared tinder** in a waterproof bag.

## Repairs

- **Duct tape** wrapped round a pole rather than a full roll.
- **Cord** — a few metres.
- **Safety pins**, a needle and thread.
- **Knife or multi-tool.**

## An honest note

Some popular kit is mostly reassurance. Survival playing cards with instructions printed too small to read, novelty wire saws, and fishing kits in a tin will not change the outcome of a bad day. The things that will: [telling someone your route](/articles/common-camping-mistakes-beginners-make/), a charged phone, a dry layer, and a whistle.

Weight matters. A pack so heavy it ends the trip early has its own risk.
