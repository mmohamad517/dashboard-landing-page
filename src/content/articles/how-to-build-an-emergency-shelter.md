Most people building an emergency shelter start with the roof. That is the wrong order. Cold ground pulls heat out of you far faster than cold air does, so what goes underneath you matters more than what goes over you.

## Insulate the floor first

Anything dry and thick: leaves, bracken, pine boughs, dry grass, your empty pack, a coiled rope. Aim for more than looks sensible — it compresses under your weight, and compressed insulation stops insulating.

If you do nothing else, do this.

## Keep it small

A shelter you can fill with your own body heat beats an impressive one you cannot. Big enough to lie in, high enough to sit up if you can manage it. That is the whole specification.

## Three shelters worth knowing

**Tarp lean-to.** Run a line between two trees at chest height, hang the tarp or poncho over it, weigh the edges with rocks, and angle the low side into the wind. Ten minutes, and it handles rain and wind well.

**Debris shelter.** No tarp. Lean a ridge pole from a stump or fork to the ground, lay branches along both sides, and pile leaf litter over the whole thing — thick, far thicker than feels necessary. Slow to build, warm when done.

**Snow trench or cave.** Dig into a firm drift, not loose powder. Keep the sleeping shelf *above* the entrance so cold air drains out. Punch a vent through the roof and keep it clear.

## Where not to put it

- Not in a dry watercourse. [Flash floods](/articles/flash-flood-safety-for-campers/) arrive with no warning under a clear sky.
- Not under a lone tall tree in a storm.
- Not at the very bottom of a valley, where cold air pools at night.
- Not under dead branches.

## Pick the site before you build

Ten minutes choosing well saves an hour of building badly. Work through it in this order:

**Out of the wind.** Wind strips heat faster than temperature alone. The lee side of a boulder, a bank or dense trees is worth more than any amount of construction.

**Off the low ground.** Cold air sinks and pools at the bottom of hollows and valleys overnight, so a spot slightly up the slope is often several degrees warmer than the flat below it.

**Dry, and staying dry.** Look at where water will run if it rains hard. Flat sandy ground in a gully is flat and sandy because water moves through it.

**Look up.** Dead branches, loose rock, anything that can fall. This takes five seconds and people skip it.

**Near materials.** Building a debris shelter fifty metres from the leaf litter costs you daylight and energy you may not have.

## Work slowly, and stop before you sweat

This is the counter-intuitive part and it matters more than technique. Building a shelter is hard physical work, and sweating soaks your insulating layers. Wet layers in cold conditions stop insulating, so the shelter you built to stay warm in can leave you colder than if you had done less.

Strip a layer before you start. Work at a pace you can hold without sweating. Take breaks. Put the layer back on the moment you stop.

## Improvising with what you carry

You are more likely to have these than a tarp:

- **A survival bag or bivvy bag** — climb inside, insulate underneath first.
- **A rain poncho or waterproof jacket** — pegged out as a small roof, or worn while you shelter behind something.
- **Bin liners** — split open, they make a ground sheet or a roof panel; a spare one over your legs holds real warmth.
- **Your empty pack** — feet inside it is a genuine improvement.
- **Trekking poles** — ridge poles for a tarp, without needing two trees.

## Once you are in it

Stay off the ground even inside. Sit on your pack rather than the floor if the insulation underneath is thin.

Eat something. Your body generates the heat the shelter traps, and it needs fuel to do it — being cold and hungry is a worse combination than being cold and fed.

Keep something bright outside where it can be seen, and keep your whistle to hand. A shelter that hides you from the weather also hides you from anyone looking, so make the outside visible even while the inside is snug.

## The snow shelter warning

Ventilate it, always. A sealed snow shelter with a stove or even a candle inside can kill you in your sleep through carbon monoxide. Keep the vent clear through the night, and work slowly while digging — sweating into your layers is the fastest way to lose the night.

## Quick answers

**How do you build an emergency shelter in the wild?**
Prioritise getting off the ground and out of the wind over building anything elaborate — insulation underneath you and a windbreak matter more than a fully enclosed structure.

**What is the biggest danger with snow shelters?**
Carbon monoxide from any flame or stove used inside without ventilation. Always keep a vent clear, even in a shelter that feels sealed and snug.

**How long does it take to build an emergency shelter?**
Work slowly and deliberately rather than rushing — sweating through your layers while building it is a faster way to get cold than the weather itself.

**Should an emergency shelter be visible from outside?**
Yes. Keep something bright outside and your whistle within reach — a shelter that hides you from weather also hides you from anyone searching.
