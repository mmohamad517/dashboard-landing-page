There is no single correct number, and anyone quoting one is guessing at your conditions. What matters is knowing what moves the answer and planning around refills.

## What changes how much you need

- **Heat.** The single biggest factor.
- **Effort and elevation gain.** Climbing costs far more than flat distance.
- **Humidity.** Dry air steals water you never notice losing.
- **Altitude.** Increases losses through breathing.
- **Sun exposure.** Open ridges cost more than shaded forest.
- **You.** Body size and personal sweat rate vary enormously.

A hot, exposed climb can require several times what a cool, shaded, flat walk of the same distance does.

## Plan around refills, not capacity

Carrying every litre you will need is heavy and often unnecessary. The better question is: where can I refill, how far apart are those points, and what do I need to get between them with margin?

That turns water from a weight problem into a route-planning problem — and it means carrying a way to [treat water](/articles/how-to-purify-water-in-the-wild/) rather than more bottles.

In genuinely dry country with no reliable sources, this flips: you carry it all, plus a real reserve.

## Do not ration into uselessness

This one surprises people. Water in your body is doing more good than water in your bottle.

Dehydration takes your decision-making long before it takes your legs, and bad decisions are what turn a long day into an incident. Sipping steadily through the day beats saving it for an emergency that your poor judgement then creates.

Pacing is sensible. Refusing to drink is not.

## Signs you are behind

- Dark urine, or not needing to go
- Headache
- Feeling unusually tired for the effort
- Irritability, or difficulty with simple decisions

By the time you feel properly thirsty you are already behind.

## Salt

On long, hot, sweaty days, water alone is not the whole answer — cramps are often a salt problem. Electrolyte tablets weigh nothing. Salty snacks do the same job.

## A starting point, and why it is only that

If you want a number to begin from, many walkers plan around roughly half a litre per hour of moderate walking in mild conditions, and adjust up sharply for heat, climbing and sun. Treat that as a place to start estimating, not a rule — the factors above move it a long way in both directions, and your own sweat rate is something only you can learn.

The way to learn it is to pay attention once. On your next few walks, note how much you actually drank and what the conditions were. After three or four trips you will know your own number better than any guide can tell you, and you will stop both over-carrying and running short.

## Can you drink from streams?

Not without treating it. Water that looks clean can carry organisms that make you very unwell, and there is no way to judge by sight, smell or taste.

Boiling works everywhere with no equipment or batteries and is the method to fall back on. Filters and purification tablets are lighter and faster on the trail. Filtering through cloth clears grit and does nothing to the germs — the treatment step is what makes it safe.

Take water from moving water above any obvious source of contamination where you can, and treat it regardless.

## Cold weather is where people get caught

Cold blunts thirst, so you drink far less than you need without noticing — and staying warm burns energy and water continuously. People routinely arrive at the end of a winter day more dehydrated than they would after the same walk in summer, and dehydration makes you feel colder and think worse.

Practical steps: keep a bottle somewhere you will actually reach for it, insulate it or keep it inside your jacket, store bottles upside down so any ice forms at the bottom rather than sealing the top, and make a point of drinking at every stop even when you do not feel like it.

## Hiking with children

Children dehydrate faster than adults and are much worse at noticing it, and they will rarely ask until they are already behind. Carry their water for them rather than expecting them to manage it, offer it at every stop as a matter of routine rather than waiting for them to ask, and treat a child who has gone quiet or grumpy as a child who needs water and food before anything else.

## Practical habits

- Drink before you start.
- Carry it where you will actually use it — a bladder hose or bottles on the shoulder strap beat a bottle at the bottom of your pack.
- Fill up at every reliable source, even when you are not low.
- In cold weather, keep it from freezing: bottles upside down, or inside your jacket.
